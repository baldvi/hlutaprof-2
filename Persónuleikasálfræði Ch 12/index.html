Intelligence (Greind)

12.1What intelligence is and why it matters

slides 2–3, book: Intelligence, pp. 393–394

In short: Intelligence (greind) is a fairly stable trait that is linked to personality and predicts important life outcomes. The book adds that it can be seen either as what you have learned (achievement) or as your ability to learn (aptitude).

  • Slides only Intelligence is a trait (eiginleiki) that is fairly stable over time. slide 2
  • Slides only Intelligence scores correlate with personality (Bartels et al., 2012): with Víðsýni (openness) r = 0.32, with samvinnuþýði (agreeableness) r = 0.21, and with taugaveiklun (neuroticism) r = −0.10. slide 2
  • Slides only Together, intelligence and personality explain a large part of the psychological differences between people. slide 2
  • Slides only Research shows a negative correlation between intelligence and the likelihood of death, which means that higher intelligence goes with a lower likelihood of death. slide 3
  • Slides only Intelligence is a better predictor of job success than parents' socioeconomic status (stéttarstaða, SES) or school performance (Strenze, 2007). slide 3 Compare Goleman's view, reported in the book, in 12.10: that is a different claim, not a direct contradiction.
  • Slides only Intelligence matters in many areas: Líkamleg heilsa (physical health), Andleg heilsa (mental health) and Menntun (education). slide 3
  • Book only The achievement view defines intelligence as how much knowledge a person has acquired compared with others of the same age. book p. 393
  • Book only The aptitude view defines intelligence as the ability or aptitude to learn, that is, to become educated. book p. 393
  • Book only IQ tests have often been used and interpreted as aptitude measures: for much of the past century they predicted school performance and were used to select people for educational opportunities, and they still are. book p. 393
  • Book only In one study of college undergraduates, general intelligence predicted 16 percent of the variability in grades, which is a correlation of about .40 between IQ and grades. book p. 393
  • Book only Need for achievement explained a further 11 percent of the variability in grades beyond what IQ explained (Lounsbury et al., 2003). book pp. 393–394

12.2Lay (implicit) and expert (explicit) theories

slides 4, 5, 7 · Slides only

In short: Ordinary people have their own beliefs about what intelligence is, and these beliefs matter: they can change how well students do and how people rate themselves and their children.

  • Implicit theories (Kenningar leikmanna) are judgements based on lay people's beliefs about what intelligence is. slide 4
  • Explicit theories (Kenningar fræðimanna) are testable theories of intelligence put forward by researchers. slide 4
  • A person's belief about the nature of intelligence can affect their academic performance (Blackwell et al., 2007; Jones et al., 2009; Shih, 2011). slide 5
  • Incremental theory (Vaxtarkenning) is the lay belief that intelligence is malleable (mótanleg), that is, it can be changed. slide 5
  • Entity theory (Heildarkenning) is the lay belief that intelligence is fixed and unchangeable. slide 5

Rating your own and your children's intelligence (Furnham and Valgeirsson, 2007) slide 7:

  • Men rated their own intelligence higher than women did, by 5.1 IQ points.
  • Parents rated sons higher in intelligence than daughters, with a mean difference of 3.8 points.
  • Yet few parents agreed with a statement that girls and boys differ in intelligence.
  • British and Icelandic parents did not differ, except that the British gave higher ratings in general and agreed more often that IQ tests are good measures, that intelligence is inherited and that there are race differences in intelligence.

12.3Intelligence across cultures

slides 4, 6, book: Intelligence, p. 396

In short: What counts as intelligent depends on the culture, so intelligence is best seen as the skills a particular culture values.

  • Slides only Definitions of intelligence differ between cultures, mainly among lay people. slide 4
  • Slides only In Vestræn Menning (Western culture), intelligence means ability in problem solving or acquiring knowledge. slide 6
  • Slides only In Afrísk Menning (African culture), social competence and maturity count as part of intelligent functioning. slide 6
  • Slides only Examples of such research are a study among the Baoule people in Africa (Dasen, 1984) and a study of American and German children's ideas about intelligence (Kurtz-Costes et al., 2005). slide 6
  • Book only On the islands of Micronesia, the ability to navigate the ocean and other maritime skills are seen as superior forms of intelligence. book p. 396
  • Book only Among Inuit who hunt along the shores in kayaks, being able to build a cognitive map of the complex Alaskan shoreline is a valued ability. book p. 396
  • Book only Many psychologists define culture partly as the shared notions of what counts as efficient problem solving (Wertsch & Kanner, 1992). book p. 396
  • Book only Western cultures emphasize verbal skills (written and oral) and the mathematical and spatial skills a technologically advanced culture needs, while other cultures may develop a sense of direction, knowledge of animal behaviour or a team approach to problem solving. book p. 396
  • Book only Because of this, the book says intelligence should always be viewed as made up of the skills valued in a particular culture. book p. 396
  • Book only As Western culture spreads ("globalization"), some worry the world will become a monoculture with one universally valued form of intelligence; for example, many Europeans speak several languages and see monolingual Americans as "linguistically challenged". book p. 396

12.4The psychometric approach: from Galton to Stanford-Binet

slides 8–10 · Slides only

In short: Psychometrics is about measuring people's characteristics, and intelligence testing grew out of it, from Galton's reaction-time measures to Binet's first intelligence test and Stern's IQ.

  • Psychometrics (Próffræði) is the branch of psychology concerned with measuring people's characteristics. slide 8
  • Francis Galton studied individual differences in perception and reaction time as an operational definition (aðgerðarbindingu) of intelligent functioning. slide 8
  • James McKeen Cattell was the first to use the term mental test (Sálfræðilegt próf). slide 8 Do not confuse him with Raymond Cattell (12.8).
  • Alfred Binet, together with Simon, wrote the first intelligence test. slide 8
  • The slide lists three features of the Stanford-Binet test: standardized administration, age-related norms, and use of the intelligence quotient. slide 9
  • Wilhelm Stern introduced the intelligence quotient (Greindarvísitala, IQ), calculated as IQ = MA/CA × 100. slide 9
  • Slide 10 shows sample items from the Army Alpha ("Test for Literates") and Army Beta ("Test for illiterates and foreign language speakers") tests, with a photo of people taking a test. slide 10

12.5Spearman's g and separate abilities

slide 11, book: Intelligence, p. 394

In short: Because scores on very different tests correlate, Spearman argued that one general factor, g, lies behind them all. The book adds that later tests also identified separate abilities, as in the SAT and ACT.

  • Slides only Charles Spearman proposed his theory of a general intelligence factor (general intelligence, g) in 1927. slide 11
  • Slides only Scores on many different intelligence tests correlate with each other; this is called the positive manifold. slide 11
  • Slides only The underlying explanation for this shared variation must be a general factor g, which Spearman saw as mental energy (greindar orka). slide 11
  • Slides only There are also specific abilities (s) used in particular tasks that the general factor cannot explain. slide 11
  • The book agrees that early psychologists saw intelligence as a single broad trait, often called g, and assumed people differ in how much of it they have. book p. 394
  • Book only As tests were developed, researchers began to identify separate abilities, such as verbal, memory, perceptual and arithmetic ability. book p. 394
  • Book only The SAT (Scholastic Aptitude Test) and ACT (American College Test) give multiple scores (e.g., English, Math, Reading, Science, Writing, Reasoning), so they are examples of the separate abilities idea. book p. 394
  • Book only Many believe the SAT and ACT measure aptitude, but their makers say they measure achievement; in use they predict college grade point average (GPA), so they are used as ability tests. book p. 394
  • Book only SAT and ACT scores correlate with college GPA in the range of .30, and with general intelligence measures from .48 to .82 depending on the IQ measure (Frey & Detterman, 2003). book p. 394
  • Book only Since the COVID-19 pandemic many colleges have made the ACT/SAT optional; it is unknown whether this change will be permanent. book p. 394

12.6Wechsler, deviation IQ and standard scores

slides 12–14 · Slides only

In short: With Wechsler's tests came the deviation IQ: a person's raw score is turned into a standard score on a normal curve with mean 100 and standard deviation 15.

  • David Wechsler defined intelligence as "the global capacity of a person to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his/her environment". slide 12
  • What was new in Wechsler's tests was that the same items were used to test different age groups, with the items ordered by difficulty (þyngdarröð). slide 12
  • Deviation IQ (Fráviksgreindarvísitala) is based on the normal distribution: raw scores are converted into standard scores with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation (SD) of 15. slide 12
  • This means, for example, that someone who scores two standard deviations above the mean (130) is at about the 98th percentile (prósenturöð). slide 12

The Normalkúrva greindar (normal curve of intelligence) shows how IQ scores are spread slide 13:

  • 34% of people score between 85 and 100, and 34% between 100 and 115.
  • 14% score between 70 and 85, and 14% between 115 and 130.
  • 2% score below 70, and 2% above 130.

Staðaltölur normalkúrvu (standard scores of the normal curve) put any score on a common scale slide 14:

ScoreMean (M)SD

How to calculate slide 14:

  1. First find the z score: z = (Xi − x̄)/SD, that is, the person's score minus the mean, divided by the SD.
  2. Then convert it to the standard score you want: standard score = z × SD of that scale + mean of that scale.
  3. Example: z = +1 gives an IQ of 1 × 15 + 100 = 115, and z = −1 gives −1 × 15 + 100 = 85.

12.8Cattell: fluid and crystallized intelligence

slides 20–21 · Slides only

In short: Raymond Cattell split intelligence into two kinds: what you know (crystallized) and how well you reason and learn new things (fluid).

  • Raymond Cattell (1963) proposed a two-factor theory of intelligence. slide 20
  • Crystallized intelligence (Reynslugreind) is knowledge acquired in school and through experience. slide 20
  • Fluid intelligence (Eðlisgreind) is the ability to see relations between ideas or things, and the ability to learn new things. slide 20

Slide 21 graphs mean T-scores by age (25 to 81) for two abilities, verbal ability and inductive reasoning slide 21. The slide prints no values for the points, so only the shape is described here:

  • Verbal ability starts lower, rises into middle age and then stays roughly level, dipping only slightly at the oldest ages.
  • Inductive reasoning starts higher than verbal ability, declines through adulthood and falls steeply at the oldest ages.
  • The two lines meet at about age 60; after that, inductive reasoning is below verbal ability.

12.9Hierarchical theories: Vernon and Carroll

slides 22–24 · Slides only

In short: Hierarchical theories arrange abilities in layers, with g at the top, broad abilities in the middle and many specific abilities at the bottom.

  • Hierarchical theories (Stigveldiskenningar) focus on how the units within a theory are organized. slide 22
  • Philip E. Vernon's hierarchical theory (1950) puts g at the top; below it come the major group factors labelled v:ed and k:m (plus a branch marked (i)); and below those come more specific abilities, such as creative abilities, reading, spelling, linguistic and clerical abilities, mathematical abilities, spatial abilities, mechanical information, and psychomotor and physical abilities. slide 22
  • John Carroll (1992) proposed a model of three strata (lög (stratum)) of intelligence. slide 23
  • The Cattell-Horn-Carroll model is an integration of three models. slide 23

12.10Multiple intelligences and emotional intelligence

book: Intelligence, pp. 394–396 · Book only

In short: Gardner defined intelligence broadly, which leads to many kinds of intelligence, including emotional intelligence; the idea is controversial because the abilities may simply reflect g.

  • Gardner (1983) gave a widely accepted definition: intelligence is the application of cognitive skill and knowledge to solve problems, learn and achieve goals that are valued by the individual and the culture. book p. 394
  • With such a broad definition there are many kinds of intelligence, beyond the traditional verbal, mathematical and performance distinctions. book p. 394
  • Howard Gardner's theory of multiple intelligences includes seven forms, for example interpersonal intelligence (social skills, the ability to communicate and get along with others), intrapersonal intelligence (insight into oneself, one's emotions and motives), kinesthetic intelligence (the abilities of athletes, dancers and acrobats) and musical intelligence (Gardner, 1999). book p. 394
  • Emotional intelligence was proposed by Peter Salovey and Jack Mayer (1990) and popularized by journalist Dan Goleman (1995). book p. 394
  • It was proposed to explain why some people with a lot of academic intelligence seem to lack practical intelligence, people skills or "street smarts". book p. 395
  • Goleman concluded that traditional intelligence measures predict school performance fairly well but do a rather poor job of predicting later life outcomes, such as occupational attainment, salary, professional status and quality of marriage (e.g., Vaillant, 1977); emotional intelligence, he argues, predicts these more strongly. book p. 395 This is Goleman's view as the book reports it; compare slide 3, which says intelligence predicts job success better than parental SES or school performance (Strenze, 2007). The two are different claims (IQ against SES and grades on the slide; IQ against emotional intelligence for Goleman), so this is not a direct slides-vs-book contradiction.

The five abilities of emotional intelligence book p. 395:

  1. Awareness of one's own feelings and bodily signals, and the ability to identify and distinguish one's emotions (such as the fear behind anger).
  2. Managing and regulating emotions, especially negative ones, and managing stress.
  3. Controlling one's impulses: directing attention and effort, delaying gratification and staying on task.
  4. Decoding others' social and emotional cues, listening and taking their perspective (empathy).
  5. Leadership: influencing and guiding others without making them angry or resentful, getting cooperation, negotiating and resolving conflict.
  • Gardner's idea is controversial: some researchers think the separate abilities correlate enough (they tend to occur in the same people) to justify a general factor g (e.g., Herrnstein & Murray, 1994; Petrill, 2002; Rammsayer & Brandler, 2002). book p. 395
  • Other experts accept a few broad distinctions, such as verbal and mathematical intelligence. book p. 395
  • Educators are acting on the idea: some universities consider "noncognitive" measures such as persistence, initiative and conscientiousness alongside SAT scores in admissions (Hoover, 2013), and some schools teach emotional intelligence, offer classes for those high on nonverbal intelligence, or foster character education, a form of civic intelligence. book pp. 395–396

12.11The cognitive approach and inspection time

slides 25–28, book: Intelligence, p. 397

In short: The cognitive approach studies the mental processes behind intelligence in experiments, often by measuring how fast people process information; faster processing goes with higher IQ.

  • Slides only The cognitive approach (Hugræn nálgun í greindarrannsóknum) studies intelligent functioning with experiments that examine cognitive processes. slide 25
  • Slides only It is based on reductionism (smættarhyggju). slide 25
  • Slides only It focuses on cognitive processes as the basis of intelligent functioning, instead of trying to describe the structure (samsetningu) or organization of intelligence. slide 25

Slides only Three cognitive views of what intelligence is slide 26:

  • the speed of neural impulses (hraði taugaboða) (Eysenck, 1986; Vernon, 1987);
  • specific neural activity related to intelligent functioning (Hunt, 1976; 1985);
  • the ability to keep mental representations active, especially when you are distracted (Engle et al., 1999).

Slides only Reaction time measures (Viðbragðstímamælingar) used in this research slide 27:

  • simple reaction times (Einfaldir viðbragðstímar);
  • choice reaction times (Val-viðbragðstímar);
  • lexical identification tasks (Tákndómar);
  • inspection times (Skoðunartími).
  • Inspection time is the time a person needs to make a simple discrimination between two displayed objects, for example deciding which of two lines on a screen is longer; it is measured in milliseconds. book p. 397
  • Slide 28 shows an inspection-time trial: a fixation cross (cue), then the stimulus (two lines of different length), then a mask, with 30–250 msec between stimulus and mask. slide 28
  • Book only Inspection time is highly related to standard measures of general intelligence (Osmon & Jackson, 2002). book p. 397
  • Book only It correlates with other measures of perceptual speed and decision reaction time (Jensen, 2011), and these mental speed abilities appear to contribute to higher IQ scores (Johnson & Deary, 2011). book p. 397
  • Book only Inspection time is also a sensitive leading indicator of the normal cognitive decline that comes with old age (Gregory et al., 2008). book p. 397
  • Book only A similar measure, discriminating auditory intervals that differ by only a few milliseconds, is also related to general intelligence (Rammsayer & Brandler, 2002). book p. 397
  • Book only A review of 172 studies (Sheppard & Vernon, 2008) concluded that intelligence correlates with mental speed, with smarter people generally faster on many mental tasks, which suggests that the brain's information processing is more efficient in people high on general intelligence. book p. 397

12.12The biological approach and family resemblance

slides 29–30 · Slides only

In short: Biological research links intelligence to brain activity, efficiency and size, and family data show that resemblance is generally higher for closer genetic relatives, highest of all for identical twins.

  • Studies in the biological approach (Líffræðileg nálgun á greind) have examined links between brain activity and intelligence using PET, fMRI, EEG and brain weight/thickness. slide 29
  • They have looked at activity in particular brain areas during different cognitive tasks. slide 29
  • Greater efficiency in neural signalling is linked to higher intelligence. slide 29
  • Greater thickness of certain brain areas is linked to higher intelligence. slide 29
  • Intelligence and brain size correlate at r = 0.33. slide 29

Fylgni eftir skyldleika (correlation by relatedness) graphs the degree of resemblance in intelligence for different relatives slide 30:

  • The slide prints the number of pairs and the genetic relatedness for each group, but not the bar values, so only the order of the bars is described here.
  • Raised in the same household, the bars rise in this order: parents and their adopted children (relatedness 0.0, lowest of all), adopted siblings (0.0), parents and their biological offspring (0.5), biological non-twin siblings (0.5), dizygotic twins (0.5), and monozygotic (identical) twins (1.0, highest of all).
  • Raised apart, parents and their biological offspring and biological siblings (both 0.5) have low bars of about the same height, while monozygotic twins raised apart (1.0) have a high bar, higher than dizygotic twins raised together.

12.13Environment and the Flynn effect

slide 31, book: Intelligence, pp. 396–397

In short: Environment may affect intelligence, and average IQ rose for decades (the Flynn effect); the book adds that it has been falling since around 1998 (the reverse Flynn effect).

  • Slides only Environmental factors (Áhrif umhverfis) such as socioeconomic status (stéttarstaða), within-family factors and breastfeeding (brjóstagjöf) may affect intelligence, but this is unclear. slide 31
  • The Flynn effect is the rise in IQ from one generation to the next. slide 31 book p. 396
  • Book only Average IQ scores around the world rose steadily for several decades, "at the rate of approximately one IQ point every four of five years within populations" (Flynn, 2007, 2012). "Four of five" is printed that way in the book and is probably a typo for "four or five". book p. 396
  • Book only The effect is named after Flynn, who first documented it (Flynn, 1984), and many others have confirmed it (Shakeel & Peterson, 2022). book p. 396
  • Book only Suggested explanations include better nutrition; Flynn's own explanation is better access to, and quality of, education. book p. 396
  • Book only Since around 1998, population IQ has declined, both in specific countries (Teasdale & Owen, 2008) and worldwide (Lynn & Harvey, 2008; Dutton, van der Linden, & Flynn, 2016); this is called the reverse or negative Flynn effect. book p. 396
  • Book only The decline worries researchers because a country's average IQ correlates with national well-being: gross domestic product (Gelade, 2008; Hunt & Wittmann, 2008), educational attainment and technological advancement (Rindermann, 2008). book p. 396
  • Book only Lower population IQ has also been linked to juvenile delinquency, adult crime, single parenthood and poverty (Gordon, 1997), and each IQ point lost goes with about $425 less income per year (Zagorsky, 2007). book p. 396
  • Book only Lynn and Harvey (2008) attribute the decline to increasing fertility rates, because IQ and fertility are negatively correlated: people with lower IQ scores tend to have more children (Shatz, 2008; Vining, 1982). book pp. 396–397

12.14Criticism, limitations and open debates

slide 32, book: Intelligence and Summary, pp. 397–398

In short: IQ tests are criticized for labelling children, for measuring only academic thinking, and for not explaining how thinking works; the book adds that measurement, bias, heritability and group differences are still heated debates.

Slides only Gagnrýni og takmarkanir (criticism and limitations) of intelligence testing slide 32:

  • Classifying children can create stereotypes and affect their learning opportunities.
  • Prediction is limited, because intelligence is not unchangeable.
  • Intelligence tests measure only analytical, logical, academic thinking.
  • Intelligence tests do not explain cognitive functioning (vitsmunastarf).

Book only Current debates book p. 397:

  • whether intelligence can be measured accurately;
  • whether intelligence measures are biased in favour of the dominant majority group in a culture;
  • how heritable intelligence is, and what that implies;
  • whether racial groups differ in intelligence, and whether such differences should be read as social class or economic differences.
  • Book only These issues are politicized and affect social and government policy, so they generate heated debate; personality psychologists help by doing the research for a scientific approach. book p. 397
  • Book only Herrnstein and Murray's controversial The Bell Curve (1994) drew direct responses (Fraser, 1995; Jacoby & Glauberman, 1995; Lynn, 2008); alternatives to their position include works by Sternberg (1985), Gardner (1983) and Simonton (1991). book p. 397
  • Book only The chapter summary sums up the views covered: intelligence as academic achievement versus an aptitude for learning; a history that began with intelligence as a single general trait and moves today toward a multiple intelligences view; and culture shaping which skills and achievements count as intelligence. book p. 398
