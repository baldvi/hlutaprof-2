## Physiological approaches to personality (*Lífeðlisfræði og persónuleiki*)

### 7.1Physiology and personality: the basic idea

part 1, slides 1–3; book pp. 185–190

**In short:** The physiological approach tries to explain differences in personality traits and behaviour by differences in how the body works, mostly the nervous system and the heart and blood vessels.

- The approach (*Tengsl lífeðlisfræði og persónuleika*) holds that differences in personality traits and behaviour can be explained by the physiological activity of the body. part 1, slide 2
- The systems studied most are the nervous system and the cardiovascular system (heart and blood vessels). part 1, slide 2 The book defines physiological characteristics as the functioning of organ systems and gives the nervous, cardiac and musculoskeletal systems as examples. book p. 186
- Book only An advantage of the approach is that physiological characteristics can be measured mechanically and reliably. book p. 186
- **Theoretical approach** (*Kenningaleg nálgun*): research starts from theory, with hypotheses that predict a link between a personality factor and a physiological variable. part 1, slide 3
- **Simplicity or parsimony** (*einfaldleikinn ráðandi*): theories here often explain a lot of behaviour with very few constructs. part 1, slide 3 book p. 189
- The book's example: one theory says people take up skydiving or motorcycle racing because they lack a certain chemical in the nervous system, which gives rise to sensation seeking. book p. 189
- Book only Human nature is more complicated than that, because two people equally high on sensation seeking may satisfy the need in very different ways (an emergency room physician versus illegal gambling or reckless driving). book pp. 189–190
- Book only So most physiologically oriented psychologists would not say that "physiology is destiny": physiology is one cause among many. book p. 190
- Book only The book links this to Allport's (1937) view that personality involves "both body and mind, inextricably fused into a personal unity". book p. 190

**Brain injury and personality** Book only

- **Elliot** (Damasio, 1994): a tumour damaged part of his prefrontal cortex. His IQ, memory, language and other cognitive functions stayed normal or above normal, but his personality changed. He could not manage his time, became impulsive, lost his job, his savings and two marriages, and reported feeling little emotion. book pp. 185–186
- The lesson is that body and mind are intimately connected: the biggest change in Elliot was in his personality, not his memory, reasoning or knowledge. book p. 186
- Traumatic brain injury often reduces the ability to inhibit impulses, most likely because of disruptions between the frontal lobes (the brain's executive control centre) and other parts of the brain. book p. 186
- **Phineas Gage** (1823–1860): on September 13, 1848 an iron rod was blasted through the left frontal part of his brain. Most of his intellectual functions stayed intact, but he went from industrious, agreeable and conscientious to impulsive, aggressive, profane and unable to plan. book p. 187

Part 1 · Slide 2 Key slide The approach: differences in personality traits and behaviour explained by the body's physiological activity, mainly the nervous system and the cardiovascular system.

### 7.2The theoretical bridge

part 1, slide 4; book pp. 186–191 (Figure 7.1)

**In short:** To link a trait to physiology, a researcher must spell out which situation, acting on which trait, produces which psychological and physiological response, and which behaviour follows.

- The slide (*Tengsl lífeðlisfræði og persónuleika II*) shows the book's Figure 7.1. part 1, slide 4 book p. 190

The chain in the figure, with introversion as the example:

1. A specific environmental condition (loud parties)
2. meets a personality characteristic (introversion)
3. and produces a psychological response (overstimulation),
4. which shows up in a physiological indicator (increased heart rate)
5. and leads to a behavioural response (avoidance).

- The book's starting example: some people find loud noise very distracting, so they seek quiet places and avoid crowds. The physiological psychologist would say such a person is introverted (a psychological characteristic) because of an overly sensitive nervous system (a physiological characteristic). book pp. 187–188
- Most physiological personality psychologists today ask whether some people show more or less of a specific physiological response than others under certain conditions. book p. 190
- Book only A **theoretical bridge** is this kind of specific statement about which traits connect to which reactions under which conditions. Researchers must build one before physiology can help explain personality. book p. 191

### 7.3Digit ratio (2D:4D)

Book only book pp. 188–189

**In short:** The ratio of index-finger to ring-finger length seems to be a lifelong marker of prenatal testosterone exposure, and it correlates modestly with risk taking, sensation seeking and social dominance.

- The 2D:4D ratio is the length of the index finger (2D) divided by the length of the ring finger (4D). Below 1.0 means the ring finger is longer. book p. 188
- Women tend to have a higher digit ratio than men. book p. 188
- The ratio is set before birth by the fetus's exposure to testosterone in the womb: the more testosterone, the smaller the ratio. book p. 188
- Wacker, Mueller and Stemmler (2012) studied more than 200 young men and found a strong link between lower digit ratio and impulsive sensation seeking. book pp. 188–189
- Other studies link lower digit ratio to risk taking (riskier lotteries, more traffic violations, riskier finance careers), to competitiveness in both sexes (Bönte et al., 2017, more than 1,000 people) and to higher sports rankings. book p. 189
- The book's conclusion: the ratio is very likely a lifelong signature of prenatal testosterone and correlates modestly with risk taking, sensation seeking and social dominance; correlates are similar but somewhat weaker in women. book p. 189

### 7.5Skin conductance and cardiovascular activity

part 1, slides 5–6; book pp. 191–193

**In short:** Sweaty palms and a racing heart are both signs that the body's fight-or-flight system is active. Neuroticism goes with spontaneous skin conductance responses, and the Type A personality with large heart rate reactions to stress.

**Skin conductance** (*Rafspenna í húð*, electrodermal activity) part 1, slide 5 part 1, slide 6

- The palms have many sweat glands controlled by the **sympathetic nervous system**, the branch of the **autonomic nervous system** that prepares the body for action (fight or flight). book p. 191
- When the sympathetic system is active (anxiety, startle, anger), the glands fill with salty water. Water conducts electricity, so the skin carries a tiny current better, even before any sweat is visible. book p. 191
- Measurement: two electrodes on the palm or a finger. A very low voltage goes in at one electrode and the researcher measures how much arrives at the other. book pp. 191–192
- Responses can be triggered by sudden noises, emotional images, mental effort, pain, anxiety, fear and guilt (the lie detector uses skin conductance). book p. 192
- Book only Some people show skin conductance responses with no external stimulus. The trait most consistently linked to this nonspecific responding is **neuroticism** (Cruz & Larsen, 1995). book p. 192
- Book only Neuroticism also goes with larger and longer-lasting responses to unpleasant images (Norris, Larsen, & Cacioppo, 2007), so a person high on neuroticism seems to have a chronically activated fight-or-flight system. book p. 192

**Cardiovascular activity** (*Starfsemi hjarta og æðakerfis*) part 1, slide 5 part 1, slide 6

- Book only **Blood pressure** has two numbers: systolic (the larger, the maximum pressure when the heart contracts) and diastolic (the smaller, the resting pressure between contractions). book p. 192
- Book only **Heart rate** is given in beats per minute (BPM). It can be measured beat by beat from the interval between beats; one second means 60 BPM. book p. 192
- A rising heart rate means the body is preparing for action. It also rises with cognitive effort, such as a hard math problem. book p. 193
- Book only **Heart rate variability (HRV)** is how much heart rate changes from beat to beat. High HRV is normal and healthy, because the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches are in balance; stress, fatigue or overtraining lower it. Traits tied to stress or emotionality can correlate negatively with HRV. book p. 193
- Book only **Cardiac reactivity**: in stressful tasks (singing in front of an audience, counting backwards from 784 by 7) everyone's heart rate rises, but some people show much larger increases. book p. 193
- Book only Cardiac reactivity is associated with the **Type A personality** (impatience, competitiveness, hostility). Chronic cardiac reactivity may contribute to coronary artery disease, which may be why Type A is linked to heart disease. book p. 193

### 7.6Measuring the brain

part 1, slides 7–10; book pp. 193–199

**In short:** Brain measures cover four things: which areas are active (function), how big they are (structure), how strongly they are linked (connections) and their electrical activity (EEG).

- The slides list four brain measures under brain activity (*Heilastarfsemi*): EEG, PET, MRI and fMRI, with an example image of PET, MRI and fMRI. The PET slide shows scans labelled NORMAL, SCHIZO and DEPRESSED. part 1, slide 7 part 1, slide 8 part 1, slide 9 part 1, slide 10
- Slides ≠ book The slides list MRI as a measure of brain activity. part 1, slide 7 The book uses PET and fMRI for brain function (activation) and MRI for brain structure, meaning the volume or thickness of brain areas. book pp. 194–195
- Book only The 1990s were the "Decade of the Brain". Imaging became cheaper and better, which allowed the large samples personality research needs and created the field of **personality neuroscience**; a journal of that name started in 2018. book pp. 193–194

Book only The book's four categories of brain measures: book p. 194

| Category | What it measures | Example finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brain function | Changes in activation of regions during a task (PET, fMRI; fMRI tracks glucose use) | Neuroticism correlated with more frontal activation to 20 negative images (Canli, Mangum, & Wells, 2001) |
| Brain structure | Volume or thickness of areas (MRI) | Extraversion correlated with volume of the medial orbitofrontal cortex, a reward area (DeYoung et al., 2012, N = 116) |
| Brain connections | Strength of connections between regions | Openness linked to the default network (Beaty et al., 2016); conscientiousness to connectivity with executive control centres (Toschi et al., 2018) |
| Brain electrical activity | EEG from scalp electrodes; evoked potentials to a tone or flash | Extraversion and sensation seeking linked to diminished evoked potentials |

- Book only Neuroticism does **not** correlate with resting brain activity. It correlates with activation during tasks relevant to the trait (fear conditioning, anticipating punishment, processing negative images), which fits the theoretical bridge. book p. 195
- Book only A review of 18 studies (Servaas et al., 2013) found more activation to anxiety- or fear-evoking tasks in people high in neuroticism; DeYoung (2015) describes a theoretical bridge for each Big Five trait. book pp. 194–195
- Book only People with higher self-esteem showed more activation in reward centres when looking at images of themselves (Izuma et al., 2018). book p. 195
- Book only Structure: neuroticism correlated with the volume of regions tied to threat and punishment (DeYoung et al., 2012); conscientiousness with a thicker cortex in 578 people (Lewis et al., 2018). In studies of over 1,000 people each, all Big Five traits correlated with the volume of various regions, with small to moderate correlations. book p. 195
- Book only **Einstein's brain** weighed 1230 grams, within normal limits. In over 7,000 people total brain volume correlated .28 with general intelligence (Cox et al., 2019). The volume of the caudate nucleus, a learning area, correlates with IQ, but the direction of cause is unknown. book p. 196
- Book only The **Human Connectome Project** started in 2011 to map the brain wiring of 1,200 healthy adults. In 460 participants, stronger connectivity in more than 30 circuits correlated with more positive life outcomes (Smith, Williams, & Segerstrom, 2015); correlation does not establish causation. book pp. 197–198
- **EEG** records the brain's small electrical currents through electrodes on the scalp, during sleep, rest or a task. In the **evoked potential** technique the participant gets a stimulus such as a tone or a flash of light. book p. 199

### 7.7Hormones, neurotransmitters and other biochemical measures

part 1, slides 5–6 and 11; book pp. 199–200

**In short:** Blood and saliva samples give hormone, immune and enzyme levels that relate to personality, such as cortisol in shy children and dopamine in extraverts.

- Biochemical analyses (*Lífefnafræðilegar mælingar*) use blood (*Í blóði*) and saliva (*Í munnvatni*). part 1, slide 5 part 1, slide 6 book p. 199
- **Immune functioning** can go up and down with stress or emotions, so it may relate to personality. book p. 199
- Book only **Testosterone** has been linked to uninhibited, aggressive and risk-taking behaviour (Dabbs & Dabbs, 2000). book p. 199
- Book only **Oxytocin** is related to affiliation and bonding and is higher in extraverts than introverts (Andari, 2015). book p. 199
- **Cortisol** can be measured in saliva. part 1, slide 5 The book calls it a byproduct of the hormone noradrenaline and reports that shy children have high cortisol levels (Kagan & Snidman, 1991), suggesting they experience more stress. book p. 199
- **Monoamine oxidase (MAO)** is an enzyme in the blood that regulates neurotransmitters; it may be a causal factor in sensation seeking (see 7.15). book p. 199
- Book only **Dopamine** responds to rewarding stimuli ("liking", "wanting", "enjoyment") and promotes exploration. Evidence is growing that extraversion is related to increased dopamine function (Wacker & Smillie, 2015). book pp. 199–200

### 7.9Eysenck's arousal theory of extraversion

part 1, slides 13–15; book pp. 200–202

**In short:** Eysenck said introverts are more aroused than extraverts because a brain-stem gateway lets in too much stimulation. So introverts avoid stimulation and extraverts seek it, each trying to stay at an optimal level of arousal.

**Who is who** (*Úthverfa-Innhverfa II*) part 1, slide 15 book pp. 200–201

| High on extraversion | Low on extraversion (introverts) |
| --- | --- |
| Talk a lot, like variety, meet new people (sociable) | Quiet, withdrawn |
| Active | Seek solitude or the company of a few friends |
| Get bored easily, cannot stand routine | Want order and routine, prefer the familiar, dislike the unexpected |

- The theory comes from Eysenck's book *The Biological Basis of Personality*. part 1, slide 14 book p. 201
- Slides ≠ book Part 1, slide 13 dates the theory "Eysenck, 1976". Part 1, slide 14 and the book give 1967, the year of *The Biological Basis of Personality*. part 1, slide 13 part 1, slide 14 book p. 201
- Eysenck held that the difference comes from different **arousal levels** (*örvunarstigi*) in the **ascending reticular activating system, ARAS** (*dreif*). part 1, slide 13 book p. 201
- The ARAS is a brain-stem structure thought to control overall cortical arousal; in the 1960s it was seen as a gateway for stimulation from the senses into the cortex. book p. 201
- The introvert's ARAS lets in too much stimulation (*Að dreif innhverfra hleypi of mikilli örvun inn*), so introverts have higher resting cortical arousal and keep it in check by seeking quiet settings. Extraverts seek out more arousal. part 1, slide 13 book p. 201
- Eysenck built in Hebb's (1955) **optimal level of arousal**: a level that is just right for a task. Being underaroused (sleepy) is as bad for an exam as being overaroused (panicked). book p. 202
- The **inverted-U graph** plots performance against arousal. Performance rises with arousal ("increasing attention and interest") up to optimal arousal and optimal performance, then falls ("impaired performance because of strong anxiety"). The slide marks extraverts (*Extrovertar*) lower on the rising side and introverts (*introvertar*) higher up, near the peak. part 1, slide 14
- The book adds that because introverts have a higher baseline level of arousal, they are **above** their optimal level of arousal more often than extraverts. book p. 202 The slide's labels only show introverts further along the arousal axis than extraverts. part 1, slide 14
- So introverts' quiet, withdrawn behaviour regulates arousal downward, and extraverts' outgoing behaviour regulates it upward. book p. 202
- Book only Table 7.2 gives items from the Eysenck extraversion scale ("Are you a talkative person?"); the average college student scores about 6. book p. 201
- Book only The theory rests on a need for arousal and stimulation, not on a need to be with people. book p. 202

### 7.10Testing Eysenck: arousability and chosen stimulation

part 1, slides 16–17; book pp. 202–204

**In short:** Introverts and extraverts do not differ at rest, only in how strongly they react to moderate stimulation, so Eysenck revised the theory from arousal level to arousability.

- **Key finding** (*Úthverfa-Innhverfa III*): no difference between introverts and extraverts at rest, but a large difference in reactions to stimuli of moderate intensity. part 1, slide 16 book pp. 202–203
- With no or very mild stimulation the differences are small or nonexistent; with moderate stimulation introverts show larger or faster responses. book p. 202
- **Revision in 1985** (Eysenck & Eysenck, 1985): the real difference is **arousability** (*næmni örvunar*), the arousal response to stimulation, not the baseline arousal level. part 1, slide 16 book p. 203
- Introverts and extraverts do not differ in brain activity while sleeping or lying quietly in a dark room (Stelmack, 1990). book p. 203
- **The noise graph** (*Úthverfa-innhverfa IV*) shows pulse rate (BPM) and skin conductance at two noise intensities, "introvert choice" and "extravert choice". Introverts have higher pulse rate and skin conductance than extraverts at both levels, and both groups react more at the louder "extravert choice" level. part 1, slide 17

The book describes a related noise study by **Geen (1984)**: book pp. 203–204

1. Participants did a learning task with noise and could set the volume.
2. Extraverts chose louder noise than introverts.
3. At their own chosen levels the two groups performed equally well.
4. Introverts given the extraverts' louder level did worse, and extraverts given the introverts' quieter level also did worse.
5. This supports an optimal level of stimulation that is higher for extraverts.

- Book only Extraverts also press a button more often to change the visual display (Brebner & Cooper, 1978), and students in a noisy library reading room were more extraverted (Campbell & Hawley, 1982). book p. 203
- Book only **Lemon juice demonstration**: after 4 drops of lemon juice under the tongue, introverts typically produce more saliva than extraverts (Eysenck & Eysenck, 1967; Corcoran, 1964). book p. 203

Part 1 · Slide 16 Key slide No difference between introverts and extraverts at rest, but a large difference in reactions to moderate stimuli; the 1985 revision to arousability.

### 7.11Gray's reinforcement sensitivity theory

part 2, slides 2–3; book p. 204

**In short:** Gray proposed two brain systems: one that drives approach toward reward (BAS, the accelerator) and one that inhibits behaviour when punishment threatens (BIS, the brakes).

- **Sensitivity to reward or punishment** (*Næmni fyrir umbun eða refsingu*): reinforcement sensitivity theory (Gray, 1972). part 2, slide 2 book p. 204
- Gray based the model on brain function research with animals. book p. 204
- **Behavioural activation system, BAS** (*Kerfi um virkjun hegðunar*): the organism is receptive to cues of reward in the environment; it regulates approach behaviour. part 2, slide 2 The book adds that it probably works through the dopamine system. book p. 204
- **Behavioural inhibition system, BIS** (*Kerfi um hömlun hegðunar*): the organism is receptive to cues of punishment in the environment; it regulates avoidance behaviour. part 2, slide 2 The book adds frustration and uncertainty as triggers. book p. 204
- Book only The book's analogy: BAS is like an accelerator and BIS is like the brakes. book p. 204
- People differ in how sensitive each system is. A reactive BIS makes a person vulnerable to anxiety, fear and sadness, so BIS underlies the dimension of **anxiety**. A reactive BAS makes a person prone to positive emotions and approach, so BAS underlies **impulsivity**, the inability to inhibit behaviour. book p. 204
- Slides only **Gray's model** (*Kenning Gray*), "a conceptual nervous system for personality (adapted from Gray, 1987a)". An arousal mechanism is fed by both BAS and BIS (+) and in turn excites BAS, the consummatory mechanisms and the fight/flight system (+). BAS and BIS inhibit each other (–), BIS also inhibits the consummatory mechanisms and the fight/flight system (–), and those two inhibit each other (–). part 2, slide 3

Part 2 · Slide 2 Key slide Gray's reinforcement sensitivity theory: BAS responds to cues of reward (approach), BIS to cues of punishment (avoidance).

Part 2 · Slide 3 Gray's conceptual nervous system: arousal mechanism, BAS, BIS, consummatory mechanisms and fight/flight system, with excitatory (+) and inhibitory (–) links.

### 7.12BIS, BAS and Eysenck's dimensions

part 2, slide 4; book pp. 204–205 (Figure 7.2)

**In short:** Gray's anxiety and impulsivity axes are Eysenck's extraversion and neuroticism axes turned diagonally; today BAS is seen as close to extraversion and BIS as close to neuroticism.

- The slide (*Næmni fyrir umbun eða refsingu II*) shows the book's Figure 7.2. part 2, slide 4 book p. 205
- Eysenck's axes run from extraversion to introversion and from emotionally stable to neurotic. Gray's axes run diagonally between them. part 2, slide 4 book p. 205
- The **impulsivity** axis runs from high impulsivity (the extraverted, neurotic corner) to low impulsivity (the introverted, stable corner); BAS points to high impulsivity. part 2, slide 4
- The **anxiety** axis runs from low anxiety (the extraverted, stable corner) to high anxiety (the introverted, neurotic corner); BIS points to the anxiety axis. part 2, slide 4
- Book only The book's view is that the relation is direct: BAS is equivalent to extraversion and BIS to neuroticism. book p. 204
- Book only Extraverts are more reactive to cues of reward ("wanting"), not necessarily to consuming rewards ("liking") (Wilt & Revelle, 2017). book p. 204
- Book only Gray revised his model to place BIS much closer to neuroticism and BAS much closer to extraversion (Pickering et al., 1999). book p. 205

### 7.13Research on reward and punishment

part 2, slide 5; book pp. 205–207

**In short:** Learning from reward and learning from punishment seem to run on different brain systems, and impulsive people learn poorly from punishment because their BIS is weak.

- **Research findings** (*Niðurstöður rannsókna*): studies by Gray and others suggest that learning with positive and negative reinforcement on the one hand, and punishment on the other, are controlled by different areas of the central nervous system. part 2, slide 5 book p. 206
- Gray's idea: impulsive people (*hvatvísir*) learn less well under punishment because they have a weak BIS. part 2, slide 5 book p. 206
- Research supports this (e.g., Newman, 1987): impulsive college students, juvenile delinquents, psychopaths and criminals are deficient in learning through punishment. part 2, slide 5 book p. 206
- **The BIS/BAS scale** (*BIS/BAS kvarðinn*) was developed by Carver and White (1994). part 2, slide 5 book p. 205 Zelenski and Larsen (1999) found it one of the best measures of BIS and BAS. book p. 205
- Book only Carver and colleagues called BIS and BAS the "Big Two" personality dimensions. book p. 205
- Book only Gray: an overly sensitive BIS explains susceptibility to anxiety, fears, worry, depression, phobias, obsessions and compulsions; an overly sensitive BAS explains positive emotions and approach. book p. 205
- Book only **Stroop study** (Larsen, Chen, & Zelenski, 2003): one group earned $5 for fast, correct answers; another started with $10 and lost $5 for slow or wrong ones. BAS scores predicted better performance under reward, and BIS scores predicted better performance under punishment. book p. 206
- Book only During COVID-19, fear-driven compliance correlated with BIS, and BAS correlated with more difficulty with social isolation (Bacon et al., 2021). book pp. 206–207

Part 2 · Slide 5 Key slide Research findings: reward and punishment learning are controlled by different areas of the central nervous system, impulsive people learn less from punishment (weak BIS; Newman, 1987), and the BIS/BAS scale (Carver & White, 1994).

### 7.14Sensation seeking: origins

part 2, slides 6–7; book pp. 207–209

**In short:** Sensation seeking began with Zuckerman's sensory deprivation research: the people who coped worst with no stimulation turned out to be those who seek thrills in daily life.

- **Sensation seeking** (*Æsisækni*) is the tendency to seek out activities that involve excitement, take risks and avoid boredom. part 2, slide 6 book p. 207
- It started with Zuckerman's research on the effects of removing all sensory input (**sensory deprivation research**). part 2, slide 6 book p. 208
- It builds on Hebb's research in this area and his ideas about arousal (*örvun*). part 2, slide 6 book pp. 207–208
- Book only In a dark, silent chamber people first feel relaxed, then bored, then anxious, and the brain creates its own sensory events. Hebb's (1955) participants chose to listen over and over to a taped lecture for 6-year-olds on the dangers of alcohol. book p. 207
- Book only **Hebb's optimal level of arousal**: when underaroused an increase in arousal is rewarding; when overaroused a decrease is rewarding. This was controversial, because most researchers then thought all motives aimed at reducing tension. book pp. 207–208
- **Zuckerman's original idea**: people high on sensation seeking tolerate a lack of sensory experience (*skorti á skynreynslu*, sensory deprivation) less well, and need more stimulation to reach an optimal arousal state (*ákjósanlegu örvunarástandi*). part 2, slide 7 book p. 208
- Book only Zuckerman and Haber (1965) noticed that the participants who found deprivation most unpleasant asked for lots of sensory material and quit early. The Sensation-Seeking Scale later predicted who tolerated deprivation poorly. book p. 208
- **Correlation with extraversion**: positive and moderate, r = 0.40–0.60. part 2, slide 7 The book calls it a moderately strong positive correlation (Zuckerman & Glicksohn, 2016) and gives no figure. book p. 208

Book only The four aspects of the Sensation-Seeking Scale (Table 7.3): book p. 208

| Aspect | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| Thrill and adventure seeking | Risky outdoor sports and activities (flying, scuba diving, parachuting) |
| Experience seeking | New sensory or mental experiences through unconventional lifestyle choices |
| Disinhibition | Getting "out of control": wild parties, gambling, sexual variety |
| Boredom susceptibility | Dislike of repetition, routine and dull people |

- Book only Correlates: police officers who volunteer for riot duty and skydivers score higher; in 550 motorcycle riders sensation seeking went with speeding and stunts (Antoniazzi & Klein, 2019); high scorers volunteer for unusual studies, make riskier bets, and report more sex partners and earlier sex. book p. 209

### 7.15The biological basis: MAO

part 2, slides 8–9; book pp. 209–210

**In short:** High sensation seekers tend to have low levels of the enzyme MAO, which leaves more neurotransmitter in the synapse, so their nervous system has weaker "brakes".

- **Neurotransmitters** carry a nerve impulse across the **synapse**, the small gap between nerve cells. book p. 209 The slide (*Líffræðilegur grunnur æsisækni*) shows a stimulus, a receptor, the nerve impulse along the axon, and at the synapse vesicles releasing neurotransmitter molecules onto receptor sites of the postsynaptic neuron. part 2, slide 8
- **Monoamine oxidase (MAO)** is an enzyme (*Lífhvati*) linked to the amount of neurotransmitter at the synapses (*taugamótum*). part 2, slide 9 book p. 209
- MAO breaks the neurotransmitter down after the impulse has passed. book p. 209 So: part 2, slide 9
  - too little MAO means too much neurotransmitter (too much transmission, for example shaky fingers);
  - too much MAO means too little neurotransmitter (diminished transmission, for example clumsy movement).
- Book only The book compares the system to a turnstile that must let the right number of people through. book p. 209
- **Zuckerman's revised idea** (*Endurskoðuð hugmynd Zukerman*; slide title *Lífeðlisfræðilegur grunnur*): sensation seekers have low MAO, which gives them less inhibition in the nervous system. part 2, slide 9 book p. 210
- Book only Across studies the correlation between sensation seeking and MAO is small to moderate but consistently negative (Zuckerman, 1991b, 2005). book p. 209
- Book only Imprisoned violent offenders scored higher on sensation seeking and had lower MAO than a community sample (Skondras et al., 2004). book p. 210
- So, according to Zuckerman (1991a), sensation-seeking behaviour comes less from seeking optimal arousal and more from having too little of the biochemical brakes in the synapses. book p. 210
- Book only A recent review supports this neurotransmitter theory (Norbury & Husain, 2015). book p. 209

### 7.19Morningness–eveningness: circadian rhythms

part 2, slide 15; book pp. 212–214 (Figure 7.3)

**In short:** Whether you are a morning lark or a night owl seems to depend on the length of your inner biological day: short rhythms make morning types and long rhythms evening types.

- **Morningness–eveningness** (*Dægurgerð*): the tendency to be a morning person (*morgunhani*) or a night person (*næturhrafn*) has a biological basis. part 2, slide 15 book p. 212 The term comes from Horne and Ostberg (1976), and the book calls the types "larks" and "owls". book p. 212
- Slides ≠ book The slide says the body clock (*Líkamsklukkan*) is set by body temperature. part 2, slide 15 In the book, body temperature is a rhythm that follows the circadian cycle, and the clock is fitted to the 24-hour day by outside time cues, most notably the light–dark cycle. book pp. 213–214
- **Circadian rhythms** are biological processes that cycle about every 24 to 25 hours (*circa* "around", *dia* "day"). Body temperature peaks around mid-evening (8 to 9 p.m.) and is lowest in the early morning (around 6 a.m.). book p. 213
- Book only **Temporal-isolation design**: volunteers live for weeks or months with no time cues (no windows, meals on request). Living like this is called **free running**. book p. 213
- Book only Rhythms average 24 to 25 hours but vary widely, from 16 hours in one person to 50 hours in another (Wehr & Goodwin, 1981). book p. 213
- In real life, time cues, above all the light–dark cycle, **entrain** us to a 24-hour day, but people still differ in when their peaks come. book pp. 213–214
- Book only Someone with a 26-hour rhythm peaks late (perhaps 10 p.m.) and struggles to get up at 6; someone with a 22-hour rhythm peaks early (perhaps 6 p.m.) and wakes easily. book p. 214

### 7.20Measuring chronotype and what it predicts

part 2, slide 16; book pp. 214–217

**In short:** A questionnaire measures morningness–eveningness well: it tracks peak body temperature and daily habits, stays stable over years, and mismatched roommates get along badly.

**Individual differences** (*Dægurgerð II*) part 2, slide 16

- People differ in the length of their body clock, and this can be measured with a questionnaire.
- Morningness–eveningness is linked to the time of day when body temperature peaks.
- Slides only It is linked to activity, neuroticism and impulsivity (*Virkni, taugaveiklun og hvatvísi*); the book does not mention these three.
- It is linked to the time of best performance and to bedtime.

- Horne and Ostberg (1976, 1977) developed a **19-item questionnaire** about preferences for activities earlier or later in the day (items in Table 7.4). book pp. 214–215
- In 48 participants, scores correlated **–.51** with the time of peak body temperature. The original study was done in Sweden and has been replicated in the United States, Italy, Spain, Croatia and Japan. book p. 214
- Book only **Stability**: 90 Croatian students retested seven years later showed a significant positive correlation; the whole sample also shifted toward morningness, as expected when students start jobs (Sverko & Fabulic, 1985). book pp. 214–215
- **Validity**: over 84 days, questionnaire scores correlated strongly with rise and retire times and with the time of day students felt best (Larsen, 1985). book p. 215
- Book only **Roommates**: the bigger the difference in Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ) scores between roommates, the worse they rated their relationship (Watts, 1982). book p. 216
- Book only **Couples**: in 84 couples partners' scores correlated, and the correlation did not change with relationship length, implying selective mating on chronotype (Randler & Kretz, 2011). book p. 216
- Book only Going against one's chronotype (shift work, jet lag, an evening type getting up early) is stressful and lowers HRV. book p. 216
- The book calls this a good example of the physiological approach: a consistent behaviour pattern (time-of-day preference) based on an underlying physiological mechanism (circadian rhythm). book p. 217

### 7.21Brain asymmetry and affective style

part 2, slide 17; book pp. 217–220

**In short:** The left frontal hemisphere is more active during positive emotions and the right during negative ones, and a person's stable asymmetry predicts how strongly they react emotionally.

**Brain asymmetry and emotion** (*Ósamhverfa heila og tilfinninga*): the hemispheres (*Heilahvel*) have different roles. part 2, slide 17 book p. 217

- The left hemisphere (*Vinstra heilahvel*) is more active than the right when a person experiences positive emotions.
- The right hemisphere (*Hægra heilahvelið*) is more active when emotions are negative.
- The same pattern appears in adults, children and infants, and it is stable over time.
- It reflects reactions to stimuli of different strength (threshold for responding). part 2, slide 17
- Book only **Measurement**: EEG over the frontal lobes. **Alpha waves** (8 to 12 per second) are given off when a person is calm and relaxed, so less alpha means more activity; alpha is an inverse indicator of brain activity. book p. 217

**Key studies** book pp. 217–218

1. Adults smiling at funny films had more left frontal activation; adults showing disgust had more right activation (Davidson et al., 1990).
2. 10-month-old infants showed more left activation to a sweet solution and more right activation to a bitter one (Fox & Davidson, 1986).
3. Infants who cried when their mother left had more right-sided activation than those who did not cry (Fox & Davidson, 1987).
4. Asymmetry at 7 and 12 months was highly correlated (Fox et al., 1992); in adults test–retest correlations are .66 to .73 (Davidson, 1993, 2003).
5. Asymmetry measured at rest predicted reactions to films: right-dominant participants reported more distress to unpleasant films, left-dominant ones more pleasant reactions (Tomarken, Davidson, & Henriques, 1990; Wheeler, Davidson, & Tomarken, 1993).
6. Monkeys with more right-sided activation had higher cortisol (Davidson and colleagues, reviewed in Kosslyn et al., 2002), and so did 6-month-old infants facing a stranger; the most right-activated infants also cried more and showed more fear (Buss et al., 2003).

- Book only Frontal asymmetry works as a **moderator** of the link between an emotional stimulus and the emotional response (Coan & Allen, 2004); for example, freshmen with more right asymmetry felt more homesick (Steiner & Coan, 2011). book pp. 218–220
- **Thresholds for responding** (Fox & Calkins, 1993): a right-dominant person needs less of a negative event to feel unpleasant emotion, and a left-dominant person needs less of a positive event to feel pleasant emotion. part 2, slide 17 book p. 220
- So frontal asymmetry acts much like a traditional personality trait. book p. 220
- Book only Among right-handers, eyes drifting right while thinking suggest left-sided activation, and eyes drifting left suggest right-sided activation (Davidson, 1991); this is only a rough gauge. book p. 219

### 7.23Summary: two ways to use physiology

part 2, slide 19; book pp. 220–221 (Table 7.5)
