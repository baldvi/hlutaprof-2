Emotion and personality (Geðhrif og persónuleiki)

13.1What emotions are: components, states and traits

slides 2–3, book pp. 401–403

In short: An emotion (geðhrif) is a package of feelings, bodily changes and urges to act, and the slides add thoughts as a fourth part. Emotions can be studied as passing states or as stable traits, and personality psychology is mostly interested in the trait side.

The components of an emotion Slides ≠ book

The slides say emotions have four main parts (Geðhrif felast í fjórum meginþáttum) slide 2:

  1. Individual experiences of feelings or affect (líðan).
  2. Changes in bodily activity, for example facial reactions, breathing, muscle tension and the nervous system.
  3. Thoughts and the interpretation of the situation.
  4. An increased tendency to behave in certain ways (tilhneiging til hegðunar), called action tendencies.
  • The book defines emotions by three components only: subjective feelings (affects), bodily changes (mostly in the nervous system), and action tendencies, meaning increases in the probability of certain behaviours. The book's list has no separate "thoughts/interpretation" component book p. 402.
  • Book only The book's example is fear: it brings feelings of anxiety and panic, a faster heart rate, less blood to the digestive system and more blood to the large muscles of the arms and legs. These changes prepare the body for the action tendency of fear, which is to flee or to fight book pp. 401–402.

Why personality psychologists care Book only

  • People differ in their emotional reactions even to the same event, for example losing a wallet, so emotions help to tell people apart book p. 402.
  • Some theories stress what emotions are for. Darwin's 1872 book The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals gave a functional analysis: emotional expressions quickly tell others what is likely to happen, as when a dog bares its teeth before attacking book p. 402.

State versus trait (ástand vs eiginleiki)

  • An emotional state is a reaction to outside circumstances, while an emotional trait is a consistent way of reacting across different situations slide 3.
  • The book adds that emotional states are transitory, have a specific cause and depend more on the situation than on the person, because most people would be angry if treated unfairly book p. 403.
  • An emotional trait is a pattern of emotional reactions that a person shows consistently across a variety of life situations, for example "Mary is cheerful and enthusiastic". Like all traits, it is at least somewhat consistent across situations and at least somewhat stable over time book p. 403.

13.2Categorical versus dimensional approach

slides 3–8, book pp. 403–406 (Table 13.1, Figure 13.1)

In short: One camp looks for a small set of built-in primary emotions, but they disagree on which ones because they use different criteria. The other camp uses factor analysis of people's ratings and consistently finds two dimensions: pleasantness and activation.

Two ways to think about emotions (flokkar vs vídd)

  • The categorical approach sees emotions as a few distinct basic emotions such as anger, joy and fear; the dimensional approach places emotions along dimensions of how pleasant and how strong they are slide 3 book p. 403.

The categorical approach (Flokkunarnálgun)

  • It tries to identify the main distinct emotions of humans, which are assumed to be built into us slide 4.
  • There is no shared conclusion about which primary emotions (grunngeðhrif) exist slide 4 book p. 403.
  • The problem lies in the different criteria that researchers use to define a primary emotion slide 4 book p. 403.
  • Book only Averill (1975) collected 550 terms for feeling states; categorical researchers try to reduce this variety to a few primary emotions, much as trait research reduced thousands of adjectives to five factors book p. 403.
  • Ekman's criterion is a distinct facial expression that is recognised across cultures. People blind from birth still frown when sad and smile when happy, which suggests the expressions are not learned. Ekman's list is disgust, sadness, joy, surprise, anger and fear book p. 403. The same six are in the slide 5 table, and slide 6 shows the six faces slides 5–6.
  • Izard (1977) instead defines primary emotions by their motivational properties: fear motivates us to avoid danger, interest motivates us to learn. His criteria give 10 primary emotions slide 5 book p. 404.

The dimensional approach (Víddarnálgun)

  • It is based on empirical research rather than theoretical criteria slide 7 book p. 404.
  • People fill in questionnaires rating how much they experience many different feelings, and factor analysis (Þáttagreiningu) is used to find the dimensions underneath slide 7 book p. 404.
  • There is remarkable consensus on the result: two main dimensions (Tveir meginþættir geðhrifa), namely pleasant versus unpleasant and high versus low activation (arousal) slide 8 book p. 404.
  • When the two dimensions are drawn as axes, the emotion words fall in a circle around them, which is sometimes called the circumplex model of emotion slide 8 book p. 405, Figure 13.1.
  • So every feeling is a mix of pleasantness and arousal. Unpleasant high-arousal feelings include distressed, anxious, annoyed and hostile (the "negative affect" corner); pleasant high-arousal feelings include elated, excited, lively and enthusiastic (the "positive affect" corner); low-arousal feelings are bored and sluggish (unpleasant) or relaxed and calm (pleasant) slide 8 book p. 405, Figure 13.1.
  • Book only Some researchers still prefer categories, because anger and anxiety are both high-arousal unpleasant emotions yet have different facial expressions, feelings and action tendencies book p. 406.

13.3Content versus style of emotional life

slide 9, book pp. 406, 429

In short: Content is what emotions a person tends to have; style is how they experience them. Both behave like traits.

  • Content (innihald) refers to which emotions a person experiences slide 9 book p. 406. Calling someone cheerful describes content book p. 406.
  • Style (stíll) refers to how a person experiences emotions slide 9 book p. 406. Calling someone high on mood variability describes style, because it says their emotions change often book p. 406.
  • Both content and style can be defined as traits of emotional experience, because each shows a tendency to be stable over time and stable across situations slide 9 book p. 406.
  • The book sums it up: content is the what of emotional life and style is the how book p. 429.
  • The chapter's emotional content covers four emotional traits: happiness, anxiety, depression and anger book p. 429. The main stylistic trait is affect intensity (see 13.12).

13.4Happiness: definition and measurement

slides 10–11, book pp. 406–409 (Table 13.2)

In short: Happiness, the main pleasant emotional trait, is defined in two ways: judging one's life as satisfying, and having more positive than negative emotions. Self-reports of happiness look valid, because they agree with other kinds of evidence.

Two definitions (Jákvæð geðhrif: Hamingja og lífsánægja)

Researchers have defined happiness in two ways slide 10 book pp. 407–408:

  1. People's judgement that life is satisfying. The book calls this the cognitive life-satisfaction component: judging that one's life has purpose and meaning.
  2. Experiencing more positive than negative emotions. The book calls this the affective hedonic component: the balance of positive to negative emotions over time.
  • Book only The two components are highly correlated, so most psychologists simply speak of happiness book p. 408.
  • Book only In lists of primary emotions, happiness or joy is usually the only pleasant emotion book p. 406.

Ideas about where happiness comes from Book only

  • Aristotle: happiness is the supreme good and comes from living a virtuous life; modern researchers who stress eudaimonia, a life of meaning and purpose, follow him book p. 407.
  • Rousseau: happiness comes from satisfying one's desires and pursuing pleasure book p. 407.
  • William James: happiness is the ratio of accomplishments to aspirations, so you can raise it by accomplishing more or by lowering your aspirations book p. 407.
  • Psychologists began the serious study of happiness, also called subjective well-being, in the mid-1970s book p. 407.

Measuring happiness Book only

  • Because happiness is subjective, it is measured mostly by self-report questionnaires book p. 407.
  • Fordyce (1978) asks what percent of the time over the past six months you were happy, neutral and unhappy. College students average about 65% happy, 15% neutral and 20% unhappy (Larsen & Diener, 1985) book p. 407.
  • The Satisfaction with Life Scale (Diener et al., 1985) has five statements, such as "I am satisfied with my life", rated from 1 (strong disagreement) to 6 (strong agreement) book p. 409, Table 13.2.

Are happy people fooling themselves? Book only

  • Happiness scores do correlate with social desirability, and social desirability also correlates with peer reports of happiness. This suggests that part of being happy is having positive illusions, a somewhat inflated positive view of oneself (Taylor) book p. 408.
  • Some positive illusions help, but too much self-deception has negative effects (Schutz & Baumeister, 2017) book p. 408.
  • Self-reports still look valid: friends and family agree with them, happy people's diaries contain more pleasant experiences, clinicians agree on who is happy, and in Seidlitz and Diener's (1993) study happy people recalled more pleasant and fewer unpleasant events book p. 408.

What happy people are like (Hamingja og vellíðan)

  • Survey results on happiness and well-being (vellíðan) relate to other parts of life in the way we would expect slide 11 book p. 408.
  • Compared with unhappy people, happy people are less likely to be rude or hostile, and they report fewer health complaints slide 11 book p. 408.
  • They are more helpful, more energetic and more creative, and they find social interaction easier slide 11 book p. 408.
  • Book only The book also lists that happy people are less self-focused and more cooperative, forgiving and trusting book p. 408.

13.5Does happiness lead to success?

slide 12, book pp. 408–410

In short: Happiness goes with many good outcomes. A large meta-analysis suggests that happiness often comes before success, not just after it, and some links run both ways.

  • Book only Happiness correlates with good outcomes such as marriage quality, longevity, self-esteem and job satisfaction, and researchers usually assumed success causes happiness book pp. 408–409.
  • The question of causal direction (orsakasamhengið): does success lead to happiness, or does happiness lead to success? slide 12 book p. 409
  • A meta-analysis (Safn-rannsókn) by Lyubomirsky and colleagues (2005) suggests that happiness comes before success slide 12 book p. 409.
  • Slides only The slide gives the number of studies as 225; the book only calls it "an extremely large meta-analysis" slide 12.

Book only Two kinds of study can show the direction book p. 409:

  1. Longitudinal studies measure people at least twice. If happiness comes first and success later, causality may run from happiness to success.
  2. Experiments put half the sample in a good mood and compare an outcome with a control group.
  • Book only Longitudinal studies showed happiness comes before fulfilling work, satisfying relationships, better mental and physical health and longevity. Experiments showed happiness leads to more helping, wanting to be with others, higher self-esteem and liking of others, better immune function and better conflict resolution book p. 409.
  • Book only Reciprocal causality means causality flows both ways: happy people help more, and helping also makes people happier book pp. 409–410.

13.6Who is happy? Gender, age, ethnicity and nations

slides 13–14, book pp. 410–412 (Table 13.3)

In short: Men and women, young and old, and different ethnic groups are about equally happy, though the reasons can differ by age. Nations do differ: richer countries are happier.

  • There is no gender difference in happiness or life satisfaction slide 13 book p. 410.
  • Book only Haring, Stock and Okun (1984) analysed 146 studies and found gender explained less than 1% of the variation; Michalos (1991) found the same in 18,032 students from 39 countries book p. 410.
  • There is no difference in happiness between life stages, but the reasons for happiness may differ slide 13 book pp. 410–411:
    • Young people: success at school and work slide 13. The book also names satisfying intimate relationships book p. 410.
    • Older people: finances and health slide 13 book p. 410.
  • Book only The age finding comes from Inglehart (1990), who studied 169,776 people in 16 nations book p. 410. Healthy older people report less negative emotion than younger people, perhaps because of better emotion management (Carstensen et al., 2011) book p. 411.
  • Book only Ethnic group membership is unrelated to subjective well-being (Myers & Diener). Crocker and Major (1989) suggest disadvantaged groups keep their happiness by valuing what they are good at and comparing themselves with their own group book p. 411.
  • Nations do differ book p. 411. Slides only Slide 14 shows two rankings that are not in the 8th-edition book: an older table of country scores of average subjective well-being (Diener, Diener & Diener, 1995; labelled "Table 14.3" from an older edition), with Iceland at the top (1.11), and the World Happiness Report 2024 ranking by life evaluations in 2021–2023, with Finland, Denmark and Iceland as the top three slide 14.
  • The book's own Table 13.3 is a different table: UN data on the 20 happiest and 20 least happy countries. The constant finding is that countries with a higher standard of living and more wealth are happier book pp. 411–412. The book is inconsistent about its date: the text calls it the UN's 2022 report book p. 411, while the table caption says "based on 2019–21" and the source line says "World Happiness Report, 2019" book p. 412.

13.7Does money make people happy?

slides 15–16, book pp. 411, 413–415 (A Closer Look, Figure 13.2)

In short: Rich countries are happier than poor ones, but that may be due to third variables. Within a country, more money matters mainly when people are poor, and rising national income has not made people happier over time.

Between nations

  • Life satisfaction (lífsánægja) is higher in rich than in poor countries slide 15 book p. 413.
  • The correlation between life-satisfaction scores and gross national income (verga þjóðartekna) was 0.67 slide 15. The book gives +.67 for a nation's well-being score and its gross national product adjusted for population size (Myers & Diener, 1995) book p. 413.
  • Third-variable effects (Þriðju breytu áhrif): national wealth goes together with other things that raise well-being, so wealth itself may not be the cause. The slide asks about the health system, education and human rights slide 15. The book lists health care services, civil rights, women's rights, care for the elderly and education book p. 413.

Within countries Slides ≠ book

  • The slide says the correlation between happiness and finances is present only in poor countries slide 15.
  • The book says that in very poor countries, such as Bangladesh and India, financial status is a moderately good predictor of well-being (Diener & Diener, 1995), while in countries with a higher standard of living income has a "surprisingly weak (virtually negligible) effect" (Inglehart, 1990) book p. 413.
  • The book still reports small correlations that are "not zero": personal income and happiness +.12 in the United States (Diener and colleagues, 2010) and .20 in a German sample (Lucas & Schimmack, 2009), .19 between median hourly wage and happiness in U.S. data from 1972 to 2004 (Fischer, 2008), and .11 between income and happiness in the 2022 UN report book pp. 413–414.
  • Both agree that money matters most when people are poor. Book only The book explains this with a threshold of income: below it a person is very unlikely to be happy, but above it more money adds little book p. 413.

Over time: the Easterlin paradox

  • Slide 16 is the book's Figure 13.2: U.S. per-person after-tax income (in 1990 dollars) and the percentage describing themselves as "very happy", by year slide 16 book p. 414, Figure 13.2. The book's text says what it shows: a large increase in personal income, while the share who call themselves very happy stayed fairly constant book p. 413.
  • Book only From 1957 to the late 1990s, average after-tax income more than doubled (from $8,000 to $20,000 in 1990 dollars), yet the share of "very happy" Americans stayed around 30 percent book p. 413.
  • Book only The Easterlin paradox (Easterlin, 1974): at any one time, happiness varies with income across nations, but over time within a country, happiness does not rise as income rises book p. 413.

Other money findings Book only

  • Lottery winners were no happier than controls within six months of winning (Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman, 1978), which shows that external circumstances have a surprisingly small effect on happiness book p. 414.
  • Spending money on others raises happiness more than spending the same amount on oneself (Dunn, Aknin & Norton, 2008). Using data from 136 countries, Aknin et al. (2013) and Kushlev, Radosic and Diener (2021) showed that this pro-social spending may be universally rewarding book pp. 414–415.

13.8Negative emotions: neuroticism and its biology

slides 17–18, book pp. 419–421

In short: Frequent negative emotion is tied to the trait of neuroticism. Eysenck traced it to an easily activated limbic system and argued for a biological basis because neuroticism is stable, universal and heritable.

  • Book only Unpleasant emotions come in several distinct kinds; the book covers three with dispositional qualities: anxiety, depression and anger book p. 419.
  • Experiencing negative emotions is linked to the personality trait neuroticism/negative affect (Taugaveiklun/neikvæðar tilfinningar) slide 17 book p. 419.
  • Book only Other names for it are emotional instability, anxiety-proneness and negative affectivity book p. 420.
  • A person high on neuroticism is moody, touchy, irritable, anxious, pessimistic and complaining slide 17 book p. 420.
  • Book only Eysenck said high scorers overreact to unpleasant events and take longer to return to normal after being upset; they also worry a lot and get irritated often book p. 420.

Eysenck's biological theory

  • According to Eysenck (1967; 1990), the basis of neuroticism lies in the limbic system (Randkerfinu) slide 17 book p. 420.
  • Book only The limbic system handles emotion and the fight-or-flight reaction. If it is easily activated, the person often feels flight emotions (anxiety, fear, worry) or fight emotions (anger, irritation, annoyance) book p. 420.

Three arguments support a biological basis slide 18 book p. 420:

  1. Neuroticism is stable over time. The slide cites Soldz and Vaillant (1999); the book cites Conley, who found a high test-retest correlation after 45 years.
  2. Neuroticism is one of the major dimensions of personality, found across data sources and cultures.
  3. Heredity explains part of how people vary on neuroticism.
  • Book only Trait negative affect is fairly heritable, while trait positive affect shows a significant shared-environment component; what is inherited is probably differences in neurotransmitter function book p. 420.

Brain findings Book only

  • Emotion is linked to increased activity in the anterior cingulate cortex book p. 421.
  • Neuroticism correlates with the volume of brain regions involved in threat and punishment, and with larger and thicker prefrontal regions that regulate negative emotion book p. 421.
  • When asked to dampen their reactions to unpleasant images, high-neuroticism people showed more prefrontal activity, as if working harder to regulate (Harenski, Kim & Hamann, 2009) book p. 421.
  • High-neuroticism people show slower recovery of amygdala activity after unpleasant images, so their negative emotions last longer (Schuyler et al., 2014) book p. 421.

13.9Neuroticism: cognitive theories, health and attention

slides 19–20, book pp. 421–424

In short: Cognitive theories say high-neuroticism people process negative information about themselves preferentially. They report and remember more symptoms, but they are not more often seriously ill, although their immune response may be weaker.

Cognitive theories (Hugrænar kenningar)

  • Neuroticism comes from specialised processing of negative information in attention, thinking and memory (Lishman, 1972; Martin, Ward & Clark, 1983) slide 19 book p. 421.
  • Book only Lishman found high-N people recalled more unpleasant information but not less pleasant information. Martin, Ward and Clark found high-N people recalled more negative information about themselves but not about others book p. 421.
  • Related to this, high-N people may have larger memory networks linked to negative emotions, so negative things are easier for them to recall slide 19 book pp. 421–422. The book links this to the spreading activation idea.

Neuroticism and health

  • High-N people tick more symptoms and remember more symptoms of illness when asked (Smith et al., 1989; Larsen, 1992) slide 20 book p. 422.
  • Book only In Smith et al. (1989), neuroticism correlated about r = .4 to .5 with self-reported symptoms. In Larsen (1992), people reported symptoms daily for two months; high-N people reported more daily symptoms and, even with that controlled, recalled more symptoms afterwards book p. 422.
  • There is little or no link between neuroticism and major diseases slide 20 book p. 422. Book only Costa and McCrae (1985): "neuroticism influences perceptions of health, but not health itself" book p. 422.
  • But some studies point to weaker immune responses (slakari ónæmisviðbrögð) (Marsland et al., 2001; Augustine et al., 2008) slide 20 book pp. 422–423.
  • Book only Marsland et al. (2001): after a hepatitis B vaccination, low-neuroticism people mounted the strongest antibody response. Augustine et al. (2008): people one standard deviation above the mean on neuroticism got lung cancer on average 4.33 years earlier than people one standard deviation below it, even with smoking history and amount controlled book pp. 422–423.

Attentional bias to threat Book only

  • High-N people are thought to have a stronger behavioural inhibition system and to scan their surroundings for threat; this is an attentional bias to threatening information book p. 423.
  • It is tested with the emotional Stroop task: people name the ink colour of threat words (e.g. disease, failure) and neutral words. Slower colour naming for threat words means the words grab attention book p. 423.
  • Williams, Mathews and MacLeod (1996) reviewed more than 50 such experiments; high-N or anxious people were often slower on threat words book p. 423.

13.10Depression

Book only book pp. 424–427 (Table 13.4, Figures 13.5–13.6). Not on the slides.

In short: Depression needs both a vulnerability and a stressful event. Beck's theory puts the vulnerability in a negative way of thinking about the self, the world and the future; the biological theory points to neurotransmitters.

  • Depression is a traitlike dimension and a disorder estimated to strike 20 percent of people in the United States at some point book p. 424.
  • Diagnosis requires five or more symptoms in the same two-week period, such as depressed mood, loss of interest, weight change, sleep change, fatigue, worthlessness or guilt, poor concentration and thoughts of death book p. 424, Table 13.4.
  • Diathesis-stress model: a pre-existing vulnerability (diathesis) plus a stressful life event are both needed; neither alone causes depression book p. 424.

Beck's cognitive theory

  • The vulnerability is a cognitive schema, a way of processing and interpreting daily events, that distorts information in a negative direction book pp. 424–425.
  • The distortions apply to the cognitive triad: the self, the world and the future book p. 425.
  • Four distortions book p. 425, Figure 13.5:
    • Overgeneralizing: one failure becomes "I am a total failure".
    • Arbitrary inference: jumping to a negative conclusion without evidence.
    • Personalizing: assuming everything is your fault.
    • Catastrophizing: thinking the worst will always happen.
  • The distortions run quickly and outside awareness, producing automatic negative thoughts. Acting like a failure can create a self-fulfilling prophecy, and bad feelings create more distortions in a self-perpetuating cycle; Beck's therapy challenges the distortions book p. 426.

Biology of depression

  • The neurotransmitter theory says depression involves too little neurotransmitter at the synapses, mainly norepinephrine (noradrenaline), serotonin and, to a lesser degree, dopamine book pp. 426–427.
  • Drugs such as Prozac, Zoloft and Paxil block serotonin reuptake; not everyone responds, which suggests different kinds of depression book p. 427.
  • Exercise also helps; a 2016 meta-analysis found effects of similar size to antidepressants book p. 427.

13.11Anger proneness and hostility

Book only book pp. 427–429. Not on the slides.

In short: Hostility is a trait of reacting to everyday frustration with anger. It is linked to heart disease, and extreme violence is linked to damage in the brain's self-control areas.

  • Personality psychologists study why some people are more hostile than others in the same situation book p. 427.
  • Hostility: a tendency to respond to everyday frustrations with anger and aggression, to become irritated easily, to feel frequent resentment and to act rudely and uncooperatively (Dembrowski & Costa, 1987) book p. 427.
  • Chronic hostility is the part of the Type A behaviour pattern that contributes most to heart disease book p. 427.
  • Raine, Meloy and Bihrle (1998) found reduced prefrontal activity in especially violent murderers book p. 428.
  • Pincus (2001) found that in virtually all the serial killers he described there was some brain damage, and practically all came from severely abusive families; his data (Pincus, 1999) show that brain damage in violent criminals is most often in the prefrontal areas book p. 428.
  • Brain abnormalities are much more common in violent than in non-violent people. In an Austrian study, 66 percent of high-violence offenders had brain abnormalities, against 17 percent of low-violence offenders (Aigner et al., 2000) book p. 428.
  • The damage is mostly in the frontal lobe and to a lesser extent the temporal lobe, areas that regulate aggressive impulses; it reduces the ability to inhibit aggression book p. 429.

13.12Emotional style: affect intensity

slides 21–23, book pp. 429–433 (Figure 13.7, Table 13.5)

In short: Affect intensity (Styrkur tilfinninga) is how strongly a person typically feels emotions. High scorers feel strong, changeable emotions in both directions; low scorers feel mild, steady ones.

  • Affect intensity can be defined as an emotional style (geðhrifarstíl) (Larsen, 2009) slide 21 book p. 429.
  • People high in affect intensity experience strong and changeable emotions and react strongly to stimuli slide 21 book p. 429.
  • People low in affect intensity show little variation in their emotions, react little to stimuli and experience mild emotions slide 21 book p. 429.
  • Book only These descriptions say "typically", because rare events such as a pet's death can make almost anyone feel strongly; affect intensity is about reactions to ordinary everyday events book p. 430.
  • Slide 22 shows the book's Figure 13.7: two people's daily mood diaries, panels (a) and (b) slide 22 book p. 431, Figure 13.7.
  • Book only The book explains them: Subject A (panel a) stayed fairly stable near his baseline mood over the three months, apart from a bad week at the start of the semester; Subject B (panel b) had much more intense moods and swung often and rapidly between strong positive and strong negative moods book pp. 430–431.
  • Book only The data come from Larsen and Diener (1985), with moods recorded for 84 consecutive days book p. 430.

Measuring it Book only

  • Early studies used daily experience sampling, which is valid but takes weeks, so the questionnaire Affect Intensity Measure (AIM) was developed (Larsen & Diener, 1987) book pp. 430, 432, Table 13.5.

Neither end is simply better Book only

  • High scorers get a lot of zest and joy, but also strong negative reactions, and they report more physical symptoms such as muscle tension and headaches, because even strong positive emotions activate the sympathetic nervous system. They are not bothered by this and do not want to change book pp. 430, 432.
  • Low scorers are stable and avoid the troughs, but they miss the peaks of enthusiasm and joy book p. 432.

Research findings

  • People high in affect intensity have bigger swings in mood and emotion and tend to see everyday events as more important than low scorers do (Larsen et al., 1986) slide 23 book pp. 432–433.
  • Book only In that study (Larsen, Diener & Emmons, 1986), 62 people recorded their best and worst daily events for 56 days. Events that outside raters called "moderately good" or "moderately bad" were rated "very good" or "very bad" by high scorers book p. 432.
  • Book only Larsen (1987) found that high scorers' mood changes are both more frequent and larger book p. 433.
  • Slides only Affect intensity is linked to personality traits such as sociability and a high activity level, and high scorers are classed as high on extraversion and neuroticism slide 23.

13.13How content and style combine

slide 24, book pp. 433–434 (Figure 13.8)

In short: Hedonic balance (content) and affect intensity (style) are unrelated, so combining them gives four distinct kinds of emotional life.

  • Book only Content is captured by hedonic balance, the ratio of pleasant to unpleasant emotions over time. The average college student had a positive balance on 7 out of 10 days, but individuals ranged from 20 percent to 95 percent positive days (Larsen, 2000b) book p. 433.
  • Book only Hedonic balance and affect intensity are unrelated to each other (Larsen & Diener, 1985), so all four combinations exist book p. 433.

13.14Personality and well-being

slides 25–29, book pp. 411, 414–419 (Figures 13.3–13.4)

In short: Demographic facts explain little of who is happy, while extraversion and neuroticism explain much more. Mood-induction experiments suggest personality acts directly, by amplifying people's reactions to the same events.

  • Demographic variables (Lýðfræðilegar breytur) such as gender, age, ethnicity and income explain only about 10 to 15% of the variation in well-being (Costa & McCrae, 1980; Myers & Diener, 1995) slide 25 book p. 415.
  • Book only Happiness is stable: in a study of 5,000 adults, people who were happy in 1973 were also happy 10 years later (Costa, McCrae & Zonderman, 1987) book p. 411.
  • High extraversion (úthverfa) and low neuroticism (taugaveiklun) explained more of the variation in well-being than gender, ethnicity, age and other demographic variables did (Costa & McCrae, 1980; 1991) slide 25 book p. 415. Book only The book says up to three times as much book p. 415.

Costa and McCrae's model (1980)

  • Extraversion leads to more positive affect, neuroticism leads to more negative affect, and both feed into subjective well-being slide 26 book pp. 415–416, Figure 13.3.
  • Book only Dozens of studies have replicated this, but all were correlational, so they cannot show the causal path book p. 415.

Mood-induction evidence (tilfinningamyndun)

  • Research by Larsen and colleagues (1999; 2000) on mood induction supports a direct link between personality and well-being slide 28 book p. 416.
  • Book only Participants listened to guided imagery (a walk on the beach, a friend dying) or looked at pleasant or unpleasant pictures, with mood measured before and after book p. 416.
  • Extraversion was the best predictor of reactions to a positive mood induction slide 28 book p. 416.
  • Neuroticism was the best predictor of reactions to a negative mood induction slide 28 book p. 416.
  • So how you react to positive or negative emotional stimuli depends on personality: it is easy to put extraverts in a good mood and easy to put high-neuroticism people in a bad mood slide 29 book p. 416.
  • This suggests personality has a direct effect on people's emotions or well-being slide 29 book p. 418. Book only The book describes personality as an amplifier of life events book pp. 416–418.
  • Book only Not every lab finds the extraversion effect: Lucas and Baird (2004), using stand-up comedy clips, did not find that extraversion predicted a larger positive response, "at least not in two out of four studies", while the fourth study did find the effect. Smillie et al. (2012) showed across five experiments that extraverts react more strongly to rewards and desired outcomes (money, success, tasty food), not to things that are merely pleasant book p. 418.

A program to increase happiness Book only

Psychologists believe happiness must be worked at; the book summarises advice from Buss, Fordyce and Larsen in eight points book pp. 418–419:

  1. Spend time with other people.
  2. Seek challenge and meaning in work.
  3. Look for ways to help others.
  4. Take time for yourself and enjoyable activities.
  5. Stay in shape.
  6. Have a plan, but be open to new experiences.
  7. Be optimistic.
  8. Don't let things get blown out of proportion.
