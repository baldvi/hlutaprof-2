Genetics and personality (Persónuleiki og erfðir)

6.1The human genome (Erfðamengi mannsins)

slides 2–5, book p. 156

In short: The genome is all of a person's genes, packed into 23 pairs of chromosomes. Mapping the DNA sequence was a huge step, but knowing the sequence is not the same as knowing what the genes do.

  • The genome is the complete set of genes an organism possesses. book p. 156
  • Book only The human genome contains between 20,000 and 25,000 genes on 23 pairs of chromosomes. Each person inherits one set of each pair from the mother and one from the father. book p. 156
  • The slides show the 23 chromosome pairs (1–22 plus X and Y) slide 2 and how they nest: a cell contains a nucleus, the nucleus holds the chromosomes, a chromosome is made of DNA, and a gene is a stretch of that DNA. slide 3
  • Genes contain instructions for making proteins, and proteins act alone or in complexes to perform many cellular functions. slide 5
  • The goals of the Human Genome Project (mannerfðamengisáætlunarinnar) were to determine the base sequence (basaröð) of these molecules and to locate all genes and determine their characteristics. slide 4
  • Book only Scientists announced the first draft of the complete human genome on June 26, 2000. book p. 156
  • Knowing the order of the DNA molecules does not mean we know their function. slide 4 book p. 156
  • Book only Protein-coding genes are only about 2% of the genome. Parts of the other 98%, once called "genetic junk", turn out to affect humans, possibly even personality (Gibbs, 2003; Plomin, 2002). book p. 156
  • Book only Most genes are the same for everyone, which is why all humans have two eyes, a heart and so on. A small number differ between people, and some of these influence personality. book p. 156

6.2Controversy about genes and personality (Ágreiningur um gen og persónuleika)

slide 6, book p. 157

In short: Behavioral genetics has been very controversial, mainly for ideological reasons and because of fears about eugenics.

  • Behavioral genetics (Atferliserfðafræði) has been highly controversial. slide 6 book p. 157
  • One reason is ideological concerns (Hugmyndafræðileg áhyggjuefni): people worry that findings will be used or misused to support political agendas, or will lead to pessimism about the possibility of change. slide 6 book p. 157
  • The other is eugenics (Mannkynbótastefna): the notion that we can design the future of the human species by encouraging people with certain traits to reproduce and discouraging those without them. slide 6 book p. 157
  • Book only Modern researchers argue that knowledge is better than ignorance, and that a genetic component does not mean the environment is powerless to change a trait (Harden, 2021; Plomin et al., 2013). book p. 157

6.3Goals of behavioral genetics (Markmið Atferliserfðafræði)

slides 7–8, book pp. 157–159

In short: Behavioral genetics asks how much of the differences between people come from genes, how much from environment, and how the two work together.

  • Behavioral genetics tries to determine to what extent the causes of individual differences (einstaklingamun) in personality can be traced to variation in genes, environment, or the interplay of both. slide 7 book p. 157
  • Today the emphasis is more on how the interaction or correlation between genes and environment (samvirkni eða fylgni milli gena og umhverfis) determines individual differences. slide 7 book p. 159
  • Last but not least, the field tries to find out what explains environmental effects on personality: parental socialization (Félagsmótun foreldra), different teachers (Áhrif mismunandi kennara) or peer groups (Vinahópar). slide 8 book p. 159
  • Book only Height example: about 90% of individual differences in height are due to genetic differences and 10% to environment. Even so, average US adult height rose about 2 inches over the past century, most likely from better nutrition, so a largely heritable trait can still be changed by the environment. book p. 158
  • Book only Percentage of variance means that the ways people differ can be split into percentages due to different causes. book p. 159

6.4What heritability is (Hvað er arfgengi)

slide 9, book pp. 159–160

In short: Heritability is a population statistic: the share of the differences between people in a trait that comes from genetic differences.

  • Heritability (arfgengi) is the proportion of variance in a trait in a group of people that can be explained by genetic variation. slide 9 book p. 159
  • Formal definition: the proportion of phenotypic variance (svipgerð, observed differences such as height or personality) that is attributable to genotypic variance (arfgerð, differences in the genes people carry). slide 9 book p. 159
  • Book only A heritability of .50 means 50% of the observed variation is attributable to genetic variation, so the environmental component is also .50. A heritability of .20 means the environmental component is .80 (assuming no gene–environment correlation or interaction). book p. 159 book p. 160
  • Book only Environmentality is the proportion of observed variance attributable to environmental (nongenetic) differences. The larger the heritability, the smaller the environmentality, and vice versa. book p. 160

6.5What heritability is not (Hvað er arfgengi (ekki))

slide 10, book pp. 160–161

In short: Heritability says nothing about one person, it can change, and it is only a rough estimate.

  • Heritability says nothing about the individual. slide 10 You cannot say "Meredith's height is 90 percent heritable", because for one person genes and environment are inseparable. book p. 160
  • Heritability is not a constant (fasti) or unchangeable. slide 10 It applies to one population, at one time, in one range of environments, so it can be high in one population and low in another, or change over time. book p. 160
  • Heritability (erfðarstuðull) is not a precise statistic. slide 10 Measurement error distorts it, and the correlations it is computed from fluctuate from sample to sample. book p. 160
  • Book only Nature–nurture debate clarified: at the level of the individual there is no debate, just as it makes no sense to ask whether one cake is caused more by the flour or the eggs. At the level of a population, differences can be split into genetic and environmental sources. book p. 161
  • Book only Heritability of height is roughly .90, of weight roughly .50 and of mate preferences roughly .10 (Waller, 1994). So genes matter more for height, both matter about equally for weight, and environment dominates mate preferences. book p. 161

6.6Methods and selective breeding (Aðferðir atferliserfðafræðinnar)

slides 11, 19, book pp. 161–162

In short: There are four classic methods. Selective breeding shows heritability in animals but can't be used on humans.

  • The four methods are selective breeding (Ræktun eiginleika), family studies (Rannsóknir á fjölskyldum), twin studies (Tvíburarannsóknir) and adoption studies (Ættleiðingarannsóknir). slide 11 book p. 161
  • Book only Selective breeding only works if the trait is heritable. Because dog breeding has succeeded for personality traits such as boldness, aggressiveness, agreeableness and desire to please, heredity must play a role in them. book p. 162
  • Selective breeding lets us infer heritability if it works, but it is unethical to do on humans. slide 19 book p. 162

6.7Family studies (Fjölskyldu rannsóknir)

slide 12, book p. 163

In short: Family studies check whether closer relatives are more alike, but relatives share homes as well as genes.

  • Family studies compare correlations (fylgnistuðlum) of personality traits according to how closely family members are related. slide 12 book p. 163
  • Genes shared: parents and children 50%, siblings 50%, grandparents and grandchildren 25%. slide 12 book p. 163
  • Book only Uncles and aunts with nieces and nephews also share 25%, and first cousins share 12.5%. book p. 163
  • If a trait is heritable, closer relatives should be more similar than more distant ones. book p. 163
  • The problem is that people who share the same genes also share the same environment. slide 12 That is why family studies alone are never definitive. book p. 163

6.8Twin studies (Tvíburarannsóknir)

slides 13–15, book pp. 163–165

In short: Twin studies compare identical with fraternal twins. If identical twins are more alike, the trait is heritable, as long as both kinds of twins have equally similar environments.

  • Book only Identical or monozygotic (MZ) twins come from one fertilized egg that splits, so they share 100% of their genes and are always the same sex. Fraternal or dizygotic (DZ) twins come from two eggs, share 50% of their genes and can be opposite sex. Two-thirds of twins are DZ. book p. 163 book p. 164
  • Twin studies estimate heritability by comparing identical (eineggja) and fraternal (tvíeggja) twins. slide 13 book p. 163
  • If identical twins are more similar than fraternal twins, this is evidence that the trait is heritable. slide 13 book p. 164
  • Simple formula: heritability = 2(rMZ – rDZ), that is, double the difference between the two correlations. slide 13 book p. 164
    • Slide example: 2(0.80 – 0.60) = 0.40. slide 13
    • Book example, height: 2(.93 – .48) = .90, so height is 90% heritable and 10% environmental. book p. 165
  • Book only For dominance, identical twins correlate +.57 and fraternal twins only +.12 (Loehlin & Nichols, 1976). book p. 164
  • Twin studies rest on the equal environments assumption (forsendu um sambærilegt umhverfi): the environments of identical twins are not more similar than those of fraternal twins. slide 14 book p. 165
  • If parents treated identical twins more alike (for example dressing them alike), their greater similarity could come from environment instead of genes. book p. 165
  • Tests of the assumption suggest it holds (Morris-Yates, 1990). slide 15
  • Book only The book's test: twins misdiagnosed by their parents as identical or fraternal were no more or less similar than correctly labelled twins (Scarr, 1968; Scarr & Carter-Saltzman, 1979). Identical twins do dress alike and spend more time together, but that does not make their personalities more similar (Plomin et al., 2008). book p. 165

6.9Adoption studies and twins reared apart (Ættleiðingarannsóknir)

slides 16–18, book pp. 165–166

In short: Adoption studies separate genes from environment, because birth parents give only genes and adoptive parents give only environment.

  • Adoption studies compare how adopted children relate to their adoptive parents (fósturforeldrar) on one hand and to their birth parents (blóðforeldrar) on the other. slide 16 slide 18 book p. 165
  • A positive correlation between adopted children and adoptive parents is evidence of environmental influence. slide 17 book p. 165
  • A positive correlation between adopted children and birth parents is evidence of heritability. slide 17 book p. 166
  • Adoption studies get around the equal environments assumption, because birth parents provide none of the child's environment. slide 17 book p. 166
  • Problem 1, representativeness: the assumption that adoptees, adoptive parents and birth parents are typical of the population may be questionable. slide 18 book p. 166
  • Problem 2, selective placement: adoptive parents may be chosen to be too similar to the birth parents (of lík blóðforeldrum). slide 18 book p. 166
  • Slides ≠ book The slide presents both as weaknesses. The book adds that several studies confirmed the representativeness assumption holds (Plomin & DeFries, 1985; Plomin, DeFries, & Fulker, 1988) and that there does not seem to be selective placement, "so this potential problem is not a real problem in actual studies". book p. 166
  • The strongest design is twin studies of pairs separated in childhood, i.e. twins reared apart. slide 18 The book calls it one of the most powerful designs. book p. 166
  • Book only The correlation between identical twins reared apart can be read directly as heritability: a correlation of +.65 means 65% of the individual differences are heritable. book p. 166

6.11Heritability of personality traits (Arfgengi persónuleikaþátta)

slides 20–21, book pp. 167–169

In short: Most personality traits are roughly half heritable.

  • Summaries of many studies suggest that the heritability of the five-factor model (fimm þátta líkansins) is about 0.50 on average. slide 20 book p. 169
    • The slide cites Bezdjaian o.fl, 2011 and Caspi o.fl., 2005; the book cites Bouchard & Loehlin, 2001 and Caspi, Roberts, & Shiner, 2005.
  • Book only A meta-analysis of more than 100,000 participants found a slightly lower 40% (Vukasovic & Bratko, 2015); another found 48% for Neuroticism and 49% for Extraversion (van den Berg et al., 2014). book p. 169
  • Book only Swedish twin study (4,987 pairs): Extraversion MZ +.51 and DZ +.21, heritability .60; Neuroticism MZ +.50 and DZ +.23, heritability .54 (Floderus-Myrhed et al., 1980). book p. 167
  • Book only Adoption studies give somewhat lower estimates: about 40% for Extraversion and 30% for Neuroticism (Pedersen, 1993). Adoptive parent–child correlations are around zero. book p. 167
  • Activity level (Hreyfiþörf) has a heritability of 0.40 (Spinath o.fl., 2002). slide 20 book p. 167
  • Slides only Impulsivity (Hvatvísi): 0.50 (Bezdjaian o.fl. 2011). slide 20
  • Slides only Morning/evening type (Morgun/kvöld þáttur): 0.54 (Hur, o.fl., 1998). slide 20
  • Table 6.2 gives correlations between identical twins reared apart from the Minnesota Twin Study (Bouchard & McGue, 1990; Tellegen et al., 1988). Highest: absorption or imagination .74 and neuroticism .70; lowest: social closeness .15; average .54. slide 21 book p. 169
  • Book only Because these are identical twins reared apart, each correlation can be read directly as a heritability estimate (see 6.9). book p. 166
  • Book only The study examined 45 sets of identical twins and 26 sets of fraternal twins reared apart. The results startled people, because traits like traditionalism and neuroticism were thought to come from upbringing. book p. 168 book p. 169
  • Book only The same studies also show that a substantial part of the variance in personality is environmental. book p. 169

6.12Attitudes and preferences (Viðhorf og forgangsröðun)

slide 22, book pp. 169–170

In short: Some attitudes are fairly heritable, others hardly at all.

  • The heritability of attitudes varies a great deal (Allmikil dreifni í arfgengi viðhorfa). slide 22 book p. 170
  • Traditionalism (Hefðbundni) and conservatism (íhaldsemi) show relatively high heritability. slide 22 The Minnesota Twin Study found .59 for traditionalism. book p. 169
  • Religious attitudes (trúarviðhorf) show little heritability. slide 22
    • Book only One study of 400 twin pairs found heritability of essentially zero for belief in God (Loehlin & Nichols, 1976). But religiousness was 12% heritable in adolescence and 44% in adulthood (Koenig et al., 2005), so genes seem to matter more with age. book p. 170
  • Slides only The link between measures of entrepreneurship (athafnasemi/frumkvöðull) and working independently could be explained by heritability (Nicolaou og Shane (2010)). slide 22
  • Book only Twin studies of political ideologies in five countries found heritabilities of 30 to 60 percent (Hatemi et al., 2014). book p. 170
  • Book only Occupational preferences are partly genetic: 71% of their correlations with parental status were significant for genetic children but only 3% for adopted children (Ellis & Bonin, 2003). book p. 170

6.13Sexual orientation (A Closer Look)

Book only book pp. 171–172

In short: Sexual orientation shows some heritability, but well-controlled studies find it lower than early studies did.

  • Early twin and adoptive-sibling studies by Bailey and colleagues gave heritability estimates from 30 to 70 percent (Bailey et al., 1993). book p. 171
  • Critics said the samples, recruited through advertisements in gay and lesbian publications, were unrepresentative. book p. 171
  • In a large Australian twin study with random sampling (Bailey, Dunne, & Martin, 2000), concordance was 20% for identical twin men and 24% for identical twin women, lower than the 40–50% of earlier studies. book p. 172
  • Concordance is the probability that one twin is gay if the other is also gay. book p. 172
  • Childhood gender nonconformity was heritable (50% in men, 37% in women), which supports Bem's (1996) idea that it may be the inherited component of adult sexual orientation. book p. 172

6.14Drinking and smoking (Neysla áfengis og reykinga)

slide 23, book pp. 170, 172

In short: Drinking and smoking are linked to personality, stable over time and moderately heritable.

  • Drinking and smoking are linked to traits such as sensation seeking (æsisækni), extraversion (úthverfu) and neuroticism (taugaveiklun). slide 23 book p. 170
  • Use of each is stable over time. slide 23 book p. 172
  • Heritability figures on the slide: slide 23
    • Slides only Starting to smoke: 0.44 (Vink o.fl., 2005).
    • Slides only Persistent smoking (Þrálátar reykingar): 0.53 (Li o.fl. 2003).
    • Alcohol use: written "021-056" (Geels o.fl., 2012), i.e. 0.21 to 0.56.
    • Alcoholism (Alkóhólismi): +0.50 (Shuckitt, 2009).
  • Slides ≠ book For alcohol drinking the slide's range starts at 0.21 (Geels o.fl., 2012); the book says most studies show moderate heritability for both sexes, ranging from .36 to .56 (Rose, 1995). slide 23 book p. 172
  • Book only For alcoholism the book says nearly all studies show heritabilities of .50 or greater (Kendler et al., 1992), with 67% in women and 71% in men in one study (Heath et al., 1994). book p. 172
  • Book only In Australian twins, an MZ twin who smoked was about 16 times more likely to have a twin who also smoked; for DZ twins the increase was only sevenfold (Hooper et al., 1992). book p. 172

6.15Marriage and satisfaction with life (Hvað með hjónabönd?)

slide 24, book pp. 172–173

In short: Genes affect whether people marry and how satisfied they are, partly through heritable personality traits.

  • Genes influence the tendency to marry or stay single, with a heritability (erfðanleiki) of 68% (Johnson et al., 2004). slide 24 book p. 172
  • Genes explain about 50% of the variation in women's marital satisfaction (Spotts et al., 2004). slide 24 book p. 173
  • These effects may run through heritable personality traits: slide 24
    • Men: social potential and success (Félagslegir möguleikar og árangur karla). The book says married men scored higher on social potency and achievement, traits valued by women choosing partners. book p. 172 book p. 173
    • Women: warmth, positivity and low aggressiveness (Hlýleiki, jákvæðni & lágt ýgi kvenna). The book says wives' optimism, warmth and low aggressiveness explained both their own and their husbands' marital satisfaction (Spotts et al., 2005). book p. 173
  • Book only Husbands' personality did not explain as much of either partner's satisfaction. book p. 173
  • Book only Four moderately heritable factors (sense of purpose, personal growth, feeling in control of one's life, positive relationships) predict life satisfaction (Archontaki, Lewis, & Bates, 2013). book p. 173

6.16Molecular genetics (Sameindaerfðafræði)

slides 25–26, book pp. 178–180

In short: Molecular genetics hunts for specific genes linked to traits. The best-known candidate is DRD4 and novelty seeking, but each gene has only a tiny effect.

  • The aim is to identify specific genes linked to personality traits. slide 25 book p. 178
  • Book only The most common method is the association method: checking whether people with a particular gene (allele) score higher or lower on a trait. book p. 178
  • The DRD4 gene is located on the short arm of chromosome 11 (litningi nr. 11) and codes for a dopamine receptor. slide 25 book p. 178
  • DRD4 has been studied mostly for novelty seeking (nýungarsækni). People who score high seem more likely to have the longer version of the gene than the shorter one (Benjamin et al., 1996). slide 25 book p. 179
  • Book only The proposed reason: people with long DRD4 genes are relatively unresponsive to dopamine, so they seek novel experiences for a "dopamine buzz". book p. 179
  • Book only The association has replicated several times but also failed to replicate several times (Plomin & Crabbe, 2000). book p. 179
  • Book only Men with the 7-repeat (7R) allele of DRD4 invested more money in a financial risk game. The effect was statistically significant but small (Dreber et al., 2009; Figure 6.1). book p. 179
  • Book only Effects are so small that there may be 10 other equally important genes for novelty seeking, and perhaps 500 genes for other aspects of personality. book p. 179
  • Therefore findings in molecular genetics must be interpreted carefully. slide 26 book p. 179
  • Recent research has broadened to genome-wide association studies (erfðamengis rannsókna, GWAS). slide 26 book p. 180
  • Slides only These studies examine variation between people in SNIPS (DNA single nucleotide polymorphisms; spelled "SNIPS" on the slide). slide 26
  • Book only Personality seems linked to many genes, each with tiny effects, which has led to polygenic risk scores, mathematical sums of many genetic variants. book p. 180

6.17Shared versus nonshared environment (Áhrif umhverfis: Munur milli eða innan fjölskyldna)

slides 27–28, book pp. 173–175

In short: The environment matters a lot, but mostly through experiences siblings do not share.

  • If genes explain 30–50% of the variation in personality, environment must explain 50–70%. slide 27 book p. 173
  • A problem is that measurement error (mælingarvillu) must be taken into account: some differences are due neither to genes nor to environment. slide 27 book p. 173
  • Shared environmental influences (Munur milli fjölskyldna) are features siblings share, such as the books in the home, the parents' values and the schools they attend. slide 27 book p. 173
  • Nonshared environmental influences (Munur innan fjölskyldna) are features siblings experience differently, such as special treatment, different friends or summer camp. slide 27 book p. 173
  • Environmental effects come mainly from differences within families. slide 28 book p. 174
  • The correlation in personality between adopted siblings, who share environment but not genes, was 0.05. slide 28 book p. 174
  • It is not known which within-family factors matter. slide 28 book p. 174 Book only Different peer influences may be one candidate (Harris, 2007), or there may be so many environmental variables that each accounts for only a tiny fraction of the variance (Willerman, 1979). book p. 174
  • Shared-environment effects have been found for religious attitudes and for alcohol use and smoking. slide 28 book p. 174
    • Book only Genetically unrelated adoptive siblings correlate .41 (girls) and .46 (boys) in smoking and drinking (Willerman, 1979). book p. 174

6.18Genotype–environment interaction (Samvirkni arfgerðar og umhverfis)

slide 29, book pp. 175–176, 180

In short: People with different genotypes react differently to the same environment.

  • Genotype–environment interaction is the different response of individuals with different genotypes to the same environment. slide 29 book p. 175
  • Example 1: introverts (innhverfra) and extraverts (úthverfra) perform differently on tasks done in different settings. slide 29 Introverts do well in quiet rooms but poorly with distractions; extraverts do fine with noise but make many errors when the task is boring. book p. 175
  • Example 2: the role of MAOA for children raised by abusive parents (Caspi o.fl., 2003). slide 29 Abused children with a genotype producing low monoamine oxidase A (MAOA) often developed conduct disorders and antisocial, violent dispositions; those with high MAOA were far less likely to. book p. 175 book p. 176
  • Book only Similarly, childhood maltreatment or stressful life events led to persistent depression or depressive symptoms only in people with the short version of the serotonin transporter (5-HTT) gene (Uher et al., 2011). book p. 176 book p. 180

6.19Genotype–environment correlation (Fylgni arfgerðar og umhverfis)

slide 30, book pp. 176–178

In short: People with different genotypes end up in different environments, and those environments can either boost or hold back the genetic tendency.

  • Book only Genotype–environment correlation is the differential exposure of individuals with different genotypes to different environments; for example, parents give a verbally gifted child more books. book p. 176
  • Book only There are three kinds (Plomin, DeFries, and Loehlin, 1977): book p. 176 book p. 177
TypeWhat happensExample
PassiveParents provide both genes and environment; the child does nothingVerbal parents pass on verbal genes and buy many books
ReactiveOthers react to the child differently depending on its genotypeA mother keeps hugging the cuddler and hugs the noncuddler less
ActiveThe person seeks out or creates environments ("niche picking", Scarr & McCartney, 1983)High sensation seekers go skydiving
  • Genotype–environment correlation can be positive or negative: the environment can encourage or discourage the effects of the genotype. slide 30 book p. 177
  • Positive example: a positive disposition (Jákvætt upplag, optimism and cheerfulness). slide 30 Adolescents with positive emotionality evoke more affirmative regard from their parents. book p. 177
    • Slides ≠ book Minor: the slide spells the citation "Kruger o.fl., 2008"; the book has "Krueger et al., 2008". slide 30 book p. 177
  • Negative example: high or low activity in children's behavior (Buss, 1981). slide 30 Parents try to calm very active children and liven up less active ones, which opposes the trait. book p. 177
  • Book only In twins reared apart, perceived family cohesion was explained by genetic influence on Constraint and low Negative Emotionality, and a culturally stimulating family by Positive Emotionality (Krueger, Markon, & Bouchard, 2003). book p. 177 book p. 178

6.21Science, politics and values (Atferliserfðafræði, vísindi, pólitík og gildi)

slide 32, book pp. 180–181

In short: Behavioral genetics was long met with suspicion, but it is now fairly mainstream.

  • Reactions to behavioral genetics and its possibilities have been mixed. slide 32 book p. 180
  • Its findings partly contradict the environmentalist view that personality is shaped by the social environment, such as parenting. slide 32 book p. 180
  • Book only In the past century the field got a "frosty reception", because environmentalism, especially behaviorism, dominated and people recalled Nazi ideas of a master race. book p. 180
  • People feared that findings would be applied to discriminate in job choice and education. slide 32 book p. 180
  • Attitudes are more positive today, and research increases understanding of individuals and their environment. slide 32 The book says findings are now seen as fairly mainstream. book p. 181
  • Book only Science (methods for discovering what exists) can be separated from values (what people want to exist). Scientists can be biased, but the scientific method is self-correcting because its methods are public. book p. 181
